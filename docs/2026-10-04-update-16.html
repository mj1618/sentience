Update 16 · 4 October 2026

What can value do without feeling? The clearest pattern was the reverse: feeling that action did not follow

Your hypothesis says feeling is the motivator and thought works out how to serve it. This round asked what happens when something's value (how good or bad it is for you) is processed without being felt, using other people's published studies. Predictions for four views were written down first and corrected by a fairness check; two evidence gatherers collected studies without scoring them; a separate adjudicator scored (all are separate AI agents given the written plan and the evidence, not our conclusions). The clearest pattern was not the one we went looking for.

The short version

Each line is the independent adjudicator's sentence, unaltered.

  • "It is not yet established that value a person does not feel can drive even simple behaviour."
  • "In a few studies people reported wanting a food less, yet kept choosing it."
  • "In those studies the feeling was normal and the action did not follow it."
  • "This suggests that feeling by itself is not enough to produce action."
  • "It does not show that feeling is unnecessary."
  • "No study has yet tested planning in people whose feeling about the options is measurably reduced."
  • "The evidence leans modestly toward feeling being a readout of value, with a separate link from value to action."

The views, and what each predicted

Since Update 10 we have been asking you: is feeling the same thing as a value signal that thought can use, or something added to it? This round put both versions against two rivals.

ViewIn one line
IdentityFeeling just is value made available to the part of the mind that plans. The two never come apart.
Extra ingredientFeeling is something added to value, and it is what moves us. Someone who knows the values but does not feel them fails to act.
Not neededValue does the work. Someone who sees the options can plan with them whether or not they feel anything.
Affect without feelingReactions of liking and wanting can happen and motivate without being consciously felt.

The fairness check, done before any evidence, changed the design. Experiments with rewards shown too briefly to see cannot separate these views when it comes to planning: every view agrees a planner cannot use information it never received. What can separate them is people who see the options clearly but feel differently about them. "Planning" was pinned down in advance to mean specific tests, such as whether someone stops choosing a food once they have eaten their fill of it.

FINDING 1

Whether unfelt value can drive even simple behaviour is not established

  • "It is not yet established that value a person does not feel can drive even simple behaviour."
  • "The best-known finding of learning from invisible rewards did not hold up under a stricter awareness test."

All four views had predicted that unfelt value can at least drive simple behaviour such as habits. The evidence for even that is shaky. A review of 41 studies of learning to fear something unseen found "no convincing evidence". Other positive results rest on sorting people into "unaware" after the fact, which can produce false effects, or have not been repeated. The adjudicator scored this prediction undecided for every view, and noted that the "affect without feeling" view has most to lose. These verdicts rest partly on summaries of papers, not full texts.

Where the measure was reported feeling instead of whether a cue was visible, the best study (131 people) compared two drugs that block the brain chemicals dopamine or opioids with a dummy pill: effort to get food fell slightly (not for a touch reward), while ratings of wanting and liking showed no detectable change, itself only weak evidence of no change. It is one study with a small effect.

FINDING 2

Feeling present, action not following

We went looking for people who feel less and asked whether they plan worse. What turned up was the reverse case.

  • "In a few studies people reported wanting a food less, yet kept choosing it."
  • "In those studies the feeling was normal and the action did not follow it."
  • "This suggests that feeling by itself is not enough to produce action."
  • "It does not show that feeling is unnecessary."

The studies: brief magnetic stimulation of a frontal brain network in 28 people (compared with 28 others) left the drop in a food smell's pleasantness after eating intact, while choices failed to adjust. Six patients with damage to a nearby frontal region reported the normal drop in desire for a food they had eaten their fill of, and kept working for it. The adjudicator counted only the first as a full test of planning as we had defined it, and only in its first block of trials; the others are weaker or were read only in summary.

What this did to each view, in the adjudicator's scoring:

  • Identity: "lean contradicted" on its claim that feeling and value usable for planning never come apart. Feeling updated; the choice did not use it.
  • Extra ingredient: split. Its own signature case, knowing but not feeling and so not acting, has thin support.
  • Not needed: neither contradicted nor confirmed.
  • Feeling as the motivator: "strained", because normal feeling was not sufficient for action. Whether feeling is necessary was not touched.
  • "One patient who reports no fear says snakes are dangerous yet approaches them; this is a single case."

That patient is the nearest thing to support for the "extra ingredient" view: she says they are dangerous, does not feel fear, and does not act on it. One person is not a test.

FINDING 3

The study that would test this directly has not been done

  • "No study has yet tested planning in people whose feeling about the options is measurably reduced."

Every study found has at most two of the three things needed: a proper planning test, a graded report of feeling about that test's options, and a group in whom that feeling is reduced. Large studies found no link between planning scores and questionnaires about depression or apathy (1,413 people in one; another large study was mixed), but those are general questionnaires, not feeling about the options in front of the person, so the adjudicator did not count them as "feeling reduced, planning intact".

The adjudicator sketched the missing study: at least 100 people per group; let them eat one food to fullness; test whether their choices shift on combinations they were never trained on; record how pleasant and how wanted each food is at every step; and in one group give a drug intended to dull pleasure. A caution: in the evidence gathered, that drug did not reliably lower pleasure ratings (unchanged in the 131-person study), so a pilot would be needed first. Each view predicts a different pattern:

ViewPredicts
Identityknowledge of the values and feeling fall together, and planning falls in step
Extra ingredientknowledge intact, feeling flat, choices fail to shift
Not neededknowledge intact, feeling flat, choices shift normally
Affect without feelingchoices follow bodily signs of liking and effort, not the ratings

Where this leaves your hypothesis

  • "The evidence leans modestly toward feeling being a readout of value, with a separate link from value to action."

A "readout" here means a gauge that reports value without itself driving action. In the adjudicator's words, your hypothesis "must give up sufficiency; it may retain necessity (untested) and causal influence". In plain terms:

  • Give up: that feeling by itself moves the system. Feeling can be fully present and behaviour still not follow, which suggests something else connects value to action.
  • May keep, untested: that feeling is needed for planning. Not tested directly. Indirect evidence from general questionnaires leaned toward the "not needed" view.
  • May keep, with some support: that feeling influences action. A combined analysis of 91 studies found that making people feel worse raised risky, reward-seeking behaviour by a small amount (read in summary only).

Put with Update 15, where the best evidence leaned against noticed emotion being present in most moments, each of the last two rounds leaned against one strong claim: Update 15 against feeling being noticed in every moment, this round against feeling being enough by itself to produce action. Neither settles it, and both rest on few studies. What has changed is that there are now two concrete experiments, each sketched, that would test these claims directly.

On the question we keep putting to you: the "identity" answer took the harder knock this round, a lean and not a refutation; the "added" answer gained no support either.

How sure are we?

  • This is desk research. The adjudicator worked only from the two evidence briefs and re-checked no paper. Several key verdicts rest on papers read only in summary; each source in the briefs is marked with how much was read.
  • The central pattern, feeling present and action not following, rests on one study that fully meets our definition of planning, plus weaker ones.
  • "Feeling" throughout means what people report when asked. We ruled out in advance both "the feeling was there but unnoticed" and "the reported feeling was not real".
  • The identity verdict depends on a ruling: the identity view may not rescue itself by defining felt value as whatever the planner can use. The plan barred the mirror-image move in advance; the adjudicator applied it this way too. These studies also cannot say where the break lay, so the "separate link to action" is a lean.
