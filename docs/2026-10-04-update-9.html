Update 9 · 4 October 2026

Your hypothesis, checked against existing studies: not refuted, not confirmed, never put at real risk

After the audit in Update 8 said the project had drifted, this round went back to your working idea: that feeling is what motivates, and thought works out how to satisfy it. We set it against two established rival accounts, had the wording checked for fairness before any scoring, and had the evidence scored independently.

The short version

These are the independent scorer's own sentences, unaltered.

  • We tested the idea that feeling good or bad is needed for flexible, self-initiated action against two established rival accounts, using rules fixed before scoring.
  • We found no replicated study in which people acted flexibly toward a reward they demonstrably could not perceive or feel, so the idea was not refuted.
  • It was also not confirmed: no study we found removed feeling and then applied a strict test of flexible action, so the idea was never put at real risk.
  • The best-known experiments on unseen rewards show only that people squeeze harder or respond faster, and the claim that people can learn from unseen rewards did not hold up in a registered replication.
  • The evidence does not separate our idea from the view that flexible action needs an accessible representation of value, whether or not it is felt; most of the experiments hid the cue rather than removing the feeling.
  • Against Berridge's account the evidence moved slightly, if at all, in his favour, and our idea is close to a position Anthony Dickinson has held for decades.
  • One small 1991 study, in which five people worked for a morphine dose they could not distinguish from placebo, remains an untested possible counterexample. (Our note: at the lowest dose, four of the five kept working.)

The three accounts

We wrote these down first. An independent check, made without sight of the evidence, then found that we had described both rivals unfairly and that your idea, as we had worded it, could not fail. The versions below are the corrected ones that were scored.

  • Your hypothesis. Feeling good or bad is necessary for flexible, self-initiated action. Automatic behaviour (reflexes, habits, urges set off by a cue) can run on valuation that is not felt.
  • Berridge's account. The brain has a "wanting" signal that makes cues attractive and triggers pursuit. It can operate without being felt, though it is usually felt as craving. It is separate from the reactions that underlie pleasure. He does not claim that unfelt wanting drives planned action.
  • The value-representation account. Planned action runs on learned representations of how valuable things are. What matters is that the planning system can access them, not whether they are felt.

To stop "flexible" being stretched after the fact, a test was fixed in advance. Behaviour counts as flexible only if it shows at least one of: it changes when the reward is made worthless, without further feedback; it changes when the reward starts arriving whether or not the action is taken; a correct choice is made the first time between new options, or by a route never itself rewarded; or effort is weighed against reward, including choosing to decline. And "unfelt" means the person cannot detect the reward above chance and reports no change in feeling, in anticipation or on receipt; craving, urge and dread count as feeling.

What can honestly be said about your idea

The scorer was asked to set this out under four headings.

  • Supported, but only negatively. There is no replicated case of flexible, goal-directed human action driven by a reward the person demonstrably neither perceived nor felt. And where feeling is lost or blunted, self-initiated action is impaired. That second point is weak evidence, and the value-representation account predicts it equally.
  • Contradicted. Nothing contradicts the core claim. The second half of the idea, that automatic behaviour can run on unfelt valuation, is strained: with strict awareness checks, unseen rewards produced no learning, and one preregistered study found masked cues did not trigger habits either.
  • Untested. The claim that matters most: that it is the feeling that does the motivating, as distinct from the accessible representation of value that comes along with it. No study pulls those two apart.
  • How new it is. Not very. Anthony Dickinson's "hedonic interface" theory already holds that goal-directed action depends on the consciously experienced value of the outcome. Your idea is close to a restatement of it. What yours adds is the word "necessary", and the identification of that value signal with sentience itself. Neither addition has been tested. Berridge also already separates deliberate, goal-directed wanting from the cue-driven kind.

The scorer noted why so little here was decisive. The experiments on "unseen rewards" hide the cue. They do not remove the feeling; mood was not even measured. So they test whether a person needs to see the reward, which is a different question from whether they need to feel anything.

Two experiments that could refute it

Proposed by the scorer.

  1. A dose too small to feel, with a strict test. Give people two new options, one secretly paired with a low dose of a drug (an opioid in experienced users, or caffeine or nicotine) and one with a dummy. Confirm they cannot tell which is which and that their mood does not differ. Then see whether they nonetheless choose the drug option by a route that was never itself rewarded, and whether they work less for it as the effort required goes up. If they reliably do, in a preregistered, replicated study, your hypothesis is refuted.
  2. Hidden reward, visible effort, freedom to say no. Hide the size of the reward, show the effort openly, and let people accept or decline each time. If their decisions weigh the reward against the effort while trial-by-trial checks show they could not detect it, your hypothesis is refuted, and so is the value-representation account.

The scorer's report proposes no experiment that removes the feeling while leaving accessible value in place. Looking for one is the next round's task.
