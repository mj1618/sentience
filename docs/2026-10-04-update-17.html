Update 17 · 4 October 2026

What switches experience off? We know much more about what switches off responding and remembering

You asked for something broader and more fundamental. Anaesthetics are the one tool that reliably turns something off, and they are chemically unrelated to one another, so what they share should be a clue to where experience comes from. It is also the one place your field and quantum questions touch data. As before: predictions for each view were written first and corrected by a fairness check; two evidence gatherers collected published studies without scoring them; a separate adjudicator scored (all separate AI agents, given the written plan and the evidence, not our conclusions). This is desk research, and many figures below were taken from summaries of papers and have not been checked against the originals.

The short version

Each line is the independent adjudicator's sentence, unaltered.

  • "Anaesthetics reliably stop people responding and remembering; whether they stop experience is much less certain."
  • "In volunteer studies at light doses, most people woken from unresponsiveness reported having experienced something, usually dreams."
  • "No brain measurement has yet been shown to tell reliably whether an unresponsive person is experiencing anything."
  • "The old idea that anaesthetics work simply by dissolving in the fat of cell membranes has been refuted."
  • "For some injected anaesthetics a specific protein target is known; for anaesthetic gases, what they share is unknown."
  • "One mouse study found that xenon atoms with nuclear spin were weaker anaesthetics; it has not been repeated in eight years."
  • "A small brainstem area in rats strongly affects responsiveness to some anaesthetics; this comes from one laboratory and says nothing about experience."

Three things an anaesthetic can remove

We fixed one rule before looking at anything. An anaesthetic can remove responding (the person does not move or answer), remembering (they recall nothing afterwards), and experience (there was nothing it was like for them). These are different. Almost every animal study measures only the first, usually whether a rat turned onto its back rights itself. Evidence about responding is not evidence about experience unless something connects the two.

That rule turned out to decide the round.

FINDING 1

It is not clear that anaesthesia switches experience off

  • "Anaesthetics reliably stop people responding and remembering; whether they stop experience is much less certain."
  • "In volunteer studies at light doses, most people woken from unresponsiveness reported having experienced something, usually dreams."
  • "In two large studies, roughly one in twenty to one in ten surgical patients followed a command while apparently anaesthetised, and almost none remembered it."
  • "No brain measurement has yet been shown to tell reliably whether an unresponsive person is experiencing anything."

The second sentence is about volunteers held unresponsive at a steady, light dose and then woken; the doses used in surgery are deeper and have not been tested this way. The third comes from studies in which one forearm is kept free of the muscle-paralysing drug used in surgery so the patient can squeeze a hand if asked.

On brain measurements: a much-cited measure of how complex the brain's response to a magnetic pulse is does differ between drugs in the way people's later reports differ, but that rests on one group of fewer than twenty volunteers. The one test within a single drug, comparing people who then reported experience with people who did not, found no difference, with very few people in the second group. The adjudicator's wording: the sources gathered establish "much about what removes responsiveness and memory, and almost nothing about what removes experience". One reason is built in: when someone wakes and says "nothing", there is no way yet to tell nothing-happened from forgot-it.

This does not show that patients experience their surgery: the hand-squeeze tests were done just after the breathing tube was placed, before surgery began, and the dream studies used light doses. It means the question "what do anaesthetics have in common that switches experience off?" starts from an assumption that has not been shown.

FINDING 2

At the level of molecules: no single shared target

  • "The old idea that anaesthetics work simply by dissolving in the fat of cell membranes has been refuted."
  • "For some injected anaesthetics a specific protein target is known; for anaesthetic gases, what they share is unknown."

The old idea fails because some chemicals that dissolve in fat just as well do not anaesthetise at all, and because mirror-image versions of the same molecule, which dissolve identically, can differ about tenfold in strength. For two common injected drugs, changing a single building block of one protein in mice abolishes their ability to stop movement in response to pain, though the drugs still make the mice unresponsive, for a shorter time. For the inhaled gases the same change shifts sensitivity only a little, and for xenon no such test was found. A newer version of the membrane idea rests on one study that has been criticised and not repeated. Two other ideas scored "lean supported": that the gases act on the cell's energy supply (one research group; target or merely a weak point is unresolved), and on the machinery that releases signals between nerve cells (worms and flies only). The adjudicator's summary: the best-supported answer is that there is no single common molecular target. All of this concerns responding, not experience.

FINDING 3 · YOUR QUANTUM QUESTION

Two leads, neither confirmed, neither refuted

  • "One mouse study found that xenon atoms with nuclear spin were weaker anaesthetics; it has not been repeated in eight years."
  • "Studies of drugs that act on microtubules disagree about direction."

Xenon. Xenon comes in several forms that are chemically identical and differ only in the nucleus; two of them have nuclear spin, a magnetic property of the nucleus. Ordinary chemistry says that should make no difference to an anaesthetic. A 2018 study of 80 mice reported that the two forms with spin were noticeably weaker. As Update 3 reported, this was measured on top of a second anaesthetic, and the xenon-only figures appear to have been calculated, not measured. If true, it would favour accounts in which quantum effects matter for anaesthesia; it would say nothing by itself about experience. The evidence gatherer found no repeat, no failed repeat and no published criticism since, and could read only the summary of the paper. Scored: undecided, with a weak lean towards the quantum view.

Microtubules. These are tiny tubes inside nerve cells that one quantum theory of consciousness says anaesthetics act on. A 2024 study of 8 rats found that a drug which stiffens microtubules made the rats slower to go under. A 2025 study in mice by another group found the opposite: animals given a stiffening drug went under more easily, and so did animals given a drug that loosens microtubules. Cancer patients previously treated with microtubule-stiffening drugs were given about the same amount of anaesthetic gas. Scored: undecided, no lean.

The adjudicator's overall wording for quantum and field accounts: "two unrefuted, unconfirmed leads, neither about experience. Open, not supported." It also described the experiment that would settle the xenon claim: an independent laboratory, both sexes, verified pure forms of xenon, xenon given alone, coded gas cylinders so nobody knows which is which, and the plan registered in advance. A difference of a fifth or more would favour quantum accounts; a tight null result (a clear finding of no difference) would remove their best piece of evidence. This connects to Updates 1, 2 and 8, where we found no known new force strong enough to influence neurons. The xenon repeat tests something different, and is the cheapest experiment that could strengthen or remove the main evidence for quantum accounts.

FINDING 4 · YOUR FEELING-FIRST VIEW

A switch deep in the brain, or a breakdown across the cortex?

  • "A small brainstem area in rats strongly affects responsiveness to some anaesthetics; this comes from one laboratory and says nothing about experience."

The feeling-first view places the source of experience deep in the brainstem (the stalk at the base of the brain; the cortex is the folded outer layer), so it predicts that the off-switch is there. In rats, a tiny dose placed in one small brainstem area produces an unresponsive, anaesthesia-like state, and damaging that area means more drug is needed, for some drugs and not others. The positive results come from one laboratory, and a small independent trial run largely failed. Unresponsive animals can also be roused, with the drug still present, by stimulating several different places, including the front of the cortex.

On experience itself neither view was supported. The brainstem view scored undecided with a weak lean against; the rival view, that the common end-point is a breakdown of long-range communication across the cortex, scored undecided with a lean against any single whole-brain measure. In the adjudicator's words on the brainstem view: "a real brainstem lever on responsiveness for some drugs; nothing ties it to experience". It added that human brain imaging puts the change of state in the thalamus and cingulate (structures above the brainstem), not the brainstem.

What this round adds up to

We asked what switches experience off and found that the field can mostly say what switches off responding and remembering. That is a real finding, and an uncomfortable one for anyone (including us, in the plan for this round) who treats anaesthesia as the clean case of experience being absent.

For your two interests: the quantum idea has one striking, unrepeated result that a single well-run experiment could confirm or remove. The feeling-first, brainstem idea has a real lever on responsiveness and nothing yet linking it to experience.

The adjudicator named a second missing study: wake people from surgical-depth anaesthesia under two different drugs, collect at least 100 "nothing" reports, and test, with the analysis registered in advance, whether any brain measure separates them from "something" reports, while also rousing people at a constant dose. A measure that separates them well would favour the cortex-wide view; experience returning with arousal while the measure stays low would favour the brainstem view.

How sure are we?

  • This is desk research. The adjudicator worked only from the two evidence briefs and re-checked no paper.
  • Many figures were taken from summaries, or extracted from papers by a summarising tool, and have not been checked against the originals. The xenon paper was read in summary only. Treat every number on this page as unchecked.
  • The dream-report studies used light doses in volunteers. They do not show what happens at surgical depth.
  • Reports on waking cannot distinguish "no experience" from "experience, forgotten".
