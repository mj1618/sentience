# Research brief 22 — How often do people report a moment of experience with no feeling?

Evidence brief for Scenario 30, Prediction 1 only. Gathered 2026-10-04. This brief extracts
and describes; it does not score or say which account is right.

Definitions used (from the Amendment in `scenarios/30-asymmetry.md`):
- **Narrow** no-feeling = no valence/emotion reported at the moment.
- **Broad** no-feeling = additionally no bodily feeling with hedonic tone and no felt urge.
- "Neutral" answers count as no feeling. Unnoticed feeling does not count as feeling.

"What I read" codes: **FT** = full text read directly (PDF text extracted and read);
**FT-s** = full text fetched but read through an automated page summariser (numbers quoted by
the tool, not eyeballed in the original table); **ABS** = abstract/landing page only;
**SNIP** = search-result snippet only; **2ND** = number taken from a secondary source that
cites the primary.

## 2. Descriptive Experience Sampling (DES)

### 2.1 Heavey & Hurlburt (2008)
Heavey, C. L., & Hurlburt, R. T. (2008). The phenomena of inner experience. *Consciousness and
Cognition, 17*, 798–810. https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/inner-voice/2008-heavey.pdf — **FT**.

- **Sample.** 30 students (16 F, 14 M), 3 drawn at random from each decile (about 40 students
  per decile) of a screening sample stratified on SCL-90-R distress. 10 usable samples each = 300 moments. Day 1
  (6 beeps) discarded as training; 5 samples from each of days 2 and 3.
- **Probe.** Random beeper (mean 30 min). Participants "were instructed to recall, immediately
  after each beep, the details of their experience that was ongoing at the exact onset of the
  beep" and jot notes; a 1-hour expositional interview within 24 h. The interviewer then coded
  "which, if any, of the 16 forms of inner experience described in the codebook were present."
  More than one form could be coded per sample. Open, unprompted for emotion, but followed by
  detailed interview. Not a checklist.
- **Definition of Feeling (Table 1).** "Affective experiences, such as sadness, happiness, humor,
  anxiety, joy, fear, nervousness, anger, embarrassment, etc." This is the narrow definition.
- **Numbers (Table 2).** Feeling: overall 26% (female 23%, male 31%); within-participant lowest
  0% (5 of 30 people had no feeling in any sample); median 20%; highest 90% (1 person).
  Others: inner speech 26%, inner seeing 34%, unsymbolised thinking 22%, sensory awareness 22%
  (0% in 9 people; 100% in 1). Eleven other codebook forms each ≤3%.
- **Framing by authors.** They set the 26% directly against Watson (2000): "waking consciousness
  is experienced as a continuous stream of affect such that people are always experiencing some
  type of mood", calling their finding "highly discrepant from Watson's (2000) characterization
  ... but concordant with Russell's (2003) theory of core affect."
- **Not reported.** How many samples contained neither Feeling nor Sensory awareness; the overlap
  between the two; any separate count of hedonic bodily sensations or urges.

### 2.2 Heavey, Hurlburt & Lefforge (2012)
Heavey, C. L., Hurlburt, R. T., & Lefforge, N. L. (2012). Toward a phenomenology of feelings.
*Emotion, 12*, 763–777. Author manuscript:
https://hurlburt.faculty.unlv.edu/heavey-hurlburt-lefforge-2012-feelings.pdf — **FT**.

- **Sample.** No specifiable sample: "based on our DES work with hundreds of individuals over
  decades ... we do not have a specifiable sample." No new frequencies; qualitative.
- **Probe wording.** "DES asks a subject one question in many different forms ('What, if anything,
  was ongoing in your experience at the moment of the beep?')". The authors stress the
  "if anything" and that the investigator "does not specifically initiate" inquiry about any
  content type; aim is "a level playing field with respect to bodily sensations".
- **Claim.** "Much of the time people do not have feelings ... we are confident that on many and
  perhaps most occasions people do not have feelings as directly apprehended features of their
  ongoing pristine experience." Four examples of no-feeling moments are given (wondering about a
  class; thinking about an essay; noticing cold water and sounds; absorbed in TV), each described
  as "without any ... feelings."
- **What "no feeling" means in DES — bears on the "salient vs absent" question.** Verbatim: "When
  we write that 'no feelings are present,' we mean that when we and our subjects carefully examine
  the experience that directly presented itself at the specific moment, we did not discover
  feelings. It is possible that on (some of) such occasions feelings were present at a very low
  level that was not apprehended by the subjects. That is, 'no feelings are present' should be
  understood to mean, 'no feelings were directly apprehended by a process that was prepared to
  apprehend them.'" And later: "we do not contest that feelings might be exquisitely faintly
  present ... However, we think it is a large phenomenological mistake to try to differentiate
  between 'no' and 'little or no'."
  So DES does record samples as having no feeling at all (not merely "feeling not the main
  content"), but the authors concede the method cannot exclude very faint feeling.
- **Emotion process without feeling.** Sections N5–N9: cases where an emotion is judged to be
  "ongoing but not felt" (e.g. anger evident in behaviour, not in experience). The authors
  distinguish emotion process from felt feeling.
- **Bodily sensation vs feeling.** "bodily sensations are not themselves the feeling"; feelings
  sometimes include bodily sensations and sometimes do not. A bodily sensation without emotional
  significance is not coded as Feeling.
- **On definitional breadth.** The authors assert the gap with Watson "cannot be explained by
  different definitions: Watson's definition of mood ('all transient feeling states...') casts the
  net broadly ... but that definition is no broader than our understanding of pristine feelings."
  (This is the authors' own claim; the Table 1 coding definition lists emotions only.)
- **Individual differences.** "some subjects experienced (pristine) feelings nearly always,
  whereas others experienced (pristine) feelings nearly never."
- **On ESM adjective ratings.** They argue standard ESM/EMA adjective ratings lack the temporal
  precision to separate experience before the beep from experience produced by the probe.

### 2.3 Hurlburt et al. (2022) — three further DES samples, plus questionnaire comparison
Hurlburt, R. T., Heavey, C. L., Lapping-Carr, L., Krumm, A. E., Moynihan, S. A., Kaneshiro, C.,
Brouwers, V. P., Turner, D. K., & Kelsey, J. M. (2022). Measuring the frequency of
inner-experience characteristics. *Perspectives on Psychological Science, 17*(2), 559–571.
https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/inner-voice/2021-hurlburt.pdf — **FT** (main text; supplements not seen).

- DES Feeling frequency (Table 4): Study 1 **17.8%** (N = 16 subject-pool students selected
  from the top and bottom quartiles of a self-talk questionnaire, 270 samples, SD 16.9);
  Study 2 **11.8%** (N = 12 randomly chosen students, 270 samples); Study 3 **12.1%** (N = 13
  volunteers from 43 prospective clients of a community mental-health centre; 456 samples).
  Weighted mean 14.3%.
- Sensory awareness in the same studies: 33.0%, 30.3%, 27.3%.
- Three coders rated each sample; unanimous agreement on 94% of codes (Study 1).
- **Same participants' questionnaire estimates** of how often they have feelings (NIEQ):
  79.1%, 70.8%, 75.4%. Questionnaire minus DES = about 61 points (Study 1: t(15) = 9.35, d = 2.34).
  Either the questionnaire overstates or DES understates; the authors argue the former.

Heavey et al. (2019), *Frontiers in Psychology* 9:2615
(https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02615/full) — **FT-s**:
260 undergraduates self-estimated feelings at 74.3% of the time (SD 17.9) on the NIEQ.

### 2.4 Hurlburt, Alderson-Day, Fernyhough & Kühn (2015)
What goes on in the resting-state? *Frontiers in Psychology, 6*:1535.
https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.01535/full — **FT-s**.

- 5 participants. Natural environment (~91 samples): Feelings mean 29.4%, range 13–72%; Sensory
  awareness 65.6% (range 6–94%). In scanner at rest (180 samples): Feelings 8.4%, range 0–22%;
  Sensory awareness 58.8%.
- Per-participant numbers came through a summariser; not checked against the table by eye.

### 2.5 DES overall
Across five samples (76 people, ~1,570 moments) the Feeling rate is 8–29%, so narrow no-feeling
moments are roughly 71–92% of samples. DES "Feeling" = experienced emotion (narrow). DES does not
report a no-feeling rate under the broad definition.

Not found / not verified: any DES replication by a group independent of Hurlburt's lab that
reports a Feeling frequency. All five samples above involve Hurlburt as author/interviewer.

## 3. Experience sampling / EMA with rating scales or checklists

### 3.1 Trampe, Quoidbach & Taquet (2015)
Emotions in everyday life. *PLoS ONE, 10*(12): e0145450.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4689475/ — **FT-s**.

- 11,572 self-selected smartphone-app users (75% women, mean age 32.9), 65,721 reports.
- Probe: "Are you currently feeling one or several of the following emotions. If you are not
  currently feeling any of these emotions, simply click 'Next'." 18 emotions (9 positive incl.
  "alertness", 9 negative). Checklist with an explicit no-emotion route.
- **At least one emotion: 90% of reports; none: 10%.** Positive only 41%, negative only 16%,
  mixed 33%.
- Narrow definition. Checklist (leading under the scenario's rule). Participants could skip
  questionnaires. "Alertness" is on the list.

### 3.2 Neutral-affect literature (Gasper and colleagues)
Gasper, K., Spencer, L. A., & Hu, D. (2019). Does neutral affect exist? *Frontiers in Psychology,
10*:2476. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6856204/ — **FT-s**.
Gasper, K. (2023). A case for neutrality. *Affective Science*.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10514019/ — **FT-s**.

- The 2019 paper names as "Belief 1" the claim that "it is not possible to feel neutral because
  people are always feeling something" and argues against it. So the question has been framed
  explicitly in this literature.
- Cited figures (underlying papers not read): people "reported feeling neutral around 20 to 30%
  of the time" in USA/Canada, 35% in Israel/Germany, 45% in Korea (Boehnke & Schwartz 1997;
  Fordyce 1988; Furr & Funder 1998; Lee et al. 2000). These appear to be retrospective
  proportion-of-time estimates, not moment sampling — method **not verified**.
- Mesquita & Karasawa (2002, *Cognition and Emotion 16*, 127–141): share reporting no emotion in
  the preceding 3 hours — 7.5% Americans, 6.67% Japanese-Americans, 22% Japanese students in
  Japan (**2ND**, via Gasper 2019; original not read; a 3-hour window, not a moment).
- **Important caveat for scoring.** Gasper defines neutral affect as a *felt* state — "feeling
  indifferent, nothing in particular" — "the person does not feel literally nothing". Neutral
  ratings co-occurred with pleasant ratings in 59.09%/57.7% of cases and with unpleasant in
  58%/20% (Gasper et al. 2021, two studies). The 2023 review reports no study giving the
  proportion of moments that are *exclusively* neutral. So these numbers do not map cleanly on to
  "neutral = no feeling".

### 3.3 Proportion of exactly-midpoint valence ratings in ESM (Kuppens and others)
**Not found.** Several searches for ESM/EMA papers reporting the share of responses at the exact
neutral point of a bipolar valence scale or the centre of an affect grid returned nothing usable.
Kuppens et al. (2013), "The relation between valence and arousal in subjective experience"
(*Psychological Bulletin*) pools ESM datasets but I did not find a midpoint share in it (not read
in full). Zelenski & Larsen (2000) and Scherer et al. (2004) were located bibliographically only.
This is a gap: the datasets exist but the statistic appears not to be routinely published.

### 3.4 Killingsworth & Gilbert (2010)
A wandering mind is an unhappy mind. *Science, 330*, 932. — **SNIP**.
2,250 adults via iPhone app. Mind-wandering in 46.9% of samples; of those, pleasant topics 42.5%,
unpleasant 26.5%, neutral 31%. This is the valence of the *topic* of thought under a three-way
forced choice, not a report on whether any feeling was present; a separate 0–100 happiness slider
was also collected (distribution at midpoint not found).

### 3.5 Watson (2000) — the "always" claim from scale data
Watson, D. (2000). *Mood and Temperament*. Guilford. Quoted in Heavey & Hurlburt 2008 (**2ND**):
"waking consciousness is experienced as a continuous stream of affect such that people are always
experiencing some type of mood" (p. 13). Based on PANAS adjective ratings (lowest anchor "very
slightly or not at all"). I did not locate the proportion of PANAS moments with every item at the
floor.

## 4. Dreams

Sikka, P. (2020). *Dream affect: Conceptual and methodological issues in the study of emotions
and moods experienced in dreams*. Doctoral thesis, University of Turku.
https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:1394396/FULLTEXT01.pdf — **FT** (Table 9 and
surrounding text read directly). Original articles: Sikka, Valli, Virta & Revonsuo (2014),
*Consciousness and Cognition 25*, 51–66 (**ABS**); Sikka, Feilhauer, Valli & Revonsuo (2017),
*American Journal of Psychology*; Sikka, Revonsuo, Sandman, Tuominen & Valli (2018), *Journal of
Sleep Research 27*, 206–214 (**ABS**).

- **Scale.** Modified Differential Emotions Scale: 20 items (10 positive, 10 negative), each three
  adjectives, rated 0 ("I did not experience any of these feelings at all") to 4. Includes items
  such as interested/alert/curious. A dream is "affective" if any item is above 0. Checklist-style.
- **Table 9, % non-affective dreams.**
  - Study I (lab REM, 17 people, N = 115): self-rated 0.0%; external 71.3%.
  - Study II (home, 44 people, N = 552): self-rated 2.5%; external 52.2%.
  - Study III (external only): home 54.6% (N = 151); lab 70.7% (N = 120; early REM 82.2%, late
    REM 62.2%).
- **Schredl & Doll (1998)**, 133 home dreams (**2ND**, via thesis): self-rating 99.2% contain
  affect; judges' global rating 86.5%; Hall/Van de Castle content coding 42.1%.
- **Kahan & LaBerge (1996)** (**2ND**): affect in 93% by self-report vs 38% by external rating.
- **Hall (1951)** (**2ND**): dreamers rated own dreams pleasant 41%, unpleasant 25%, mixed 11%,
  **no affect 23%** (global tone question, not a checklist).
- **Fosse, Stickgold & Hobson (2001)**, The mind in REM sleep. *Sleep, 24*(8).
  https://academic.oup.com/sleep/article/24/8/1/2750033 (**ABS**): 9 subjects, 88 REM reports,
  subjects' own line-by-line ratings of discrete emotion types; "Emotions were found in 74% of 88
  mentation reports" → 26% none.
- **Hall & Van de Castle norms** (https://dreams.ucsc.edu/Norms/emotions.html; **SNIP** for the
  percentages): 500 male + 491 female student dreams, external coding of explicit emotion; no
  emotion in 66.8% (men), 49.7% (women), ~58% combined.
- **Foulkes et al. (1988); Strauch & Meier (1996)** (**SNIP**, via Domhoff): ~70% of REM reports
  had some affect on detailed questioning; 17% had no emotion where waking would have had some.
- **Merritt et al. (1994)**, *Consciousness and Cognition 3*, 46–60 (**SNIP**): 200 reports, 20
  subjects, asked to identify emotions line by line; "10-fold increase" in emotion over earlier
  methods. The per-report percentage with emotion (often quoted as ~95%) was **not verified**.
- **Sikka et al. (2021)**, *Consciousness and Cognition* (**SNIP**, unverified): self-rated
  affective in 97.9% of waking mind-wandering episodes and 94.7% of dream episodes.

Pattern across dream studies (descriptive): the no-emotion share is 0–3% with self-rated emotion
checklists, 23–30% with dreamers' own global or line-by-line judgements in three older studies,
and 50–80% when judges code spontaneous reports.

## 5. The existing debate — who has claimed what, and has it been tested?

**Claims that affect/feeling is always present**
- **Wundt** (1897): people are likely "never in a state entirely free from feeling" (quoted in
  Barrett & Bliss-Moreau 2009; **FT-s**).
- **Watson (2000)**: "people are always experiencing some type of mood" (quote in section 3.5).
- **Izard (2007)**: "there is no such thing as an affectless mind; affect or emotion is always
  present" (via Gasper 2019; **2ND**).
- **Damasio (2003)**: "we do not live in a neutral world" (via Gasper 2019; **2ND**).
- **Helson (1964)**: "All experience is more or less tinged with affect" (via Gasper 2019; **2ND**).
- **Barrett & Bliss-Moreau (2009)**, Affect as a psychological primitive. *Advances in
  Experimental Social Psychology, 41*, 167–218. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2884406/
  (**FT-s**): core affect is a basic, ever-present property of the mind; "People are often aware
  of their core affective state, although they need not be"; when in the background it "is
  perceived as a property of the world". The summariser found no empirical test of affect-free
  experience cited in the paper. A search snippet attributes to Barrett's later work the wording
  that valence and arousal "are part of every waking moment of consciousness" (**SNIP**).
- **Cleeremans & Tallon-Baudry (2022)** and the "micro-valence" literature: nothing perceived is
  ever fully neutral (**SNIP**).

**Claims that allow or assert affect-free experience**
- **Russell (2003)**, Core affect and the psychological construction of emotion. *Psychological
  Review, 110*, 145–172, p. 148 (quoted verbatim in Heavey & Hurlburt 2008): "Intense core affect
  can be the focus of consciousness, but milder core affect is typically part of the background of
  the person's conscious world ... When neutral and stable, perhaps core affect disappears
  altogether from consciousness." Elsewhere Russell holds a person is always in some core-affect
  state (**SNIP**). So: always a state, not always conscious.
- **Hurlburt, Heavey and colleagues** (2008, 2012, 2022): feelings are absent from most sampled
  moments (sections 2.1–2.3).
- **Gasper et al. (2019, 2023)**: neutral states exist and are common, but are themselves a kind of
  affect (section 3.2).

**Has anyone tested "is there experience without feeling" head-on?**
- **Yes, for the narrow definition.** Heavey & Hurlburt (2008) explicitly pit a moment-sampling
  estimate (26%) against Watson's "always" and Russell's background view. Heavey et al. (2012) call
  it "a disagreement about data that are basic to emotion science" and "discrepancies as large as
  the difference between 26% and 100%". Gasper et al. (2019) review the "always feeling something"
  belief explicitly. In dream research the self-rating vs external-rating comparison (Schredl &
  Doll 1998; Sikka 2014, 2017) is a direct methods test of how often emotion is absent. The
  project's earlier statement that nobody had tested this head-on is therefore not accurate for
  narrow feeling.
- **Not found for the broad definition.** I found no study that samples single moments with a
  neutral probe and then asks directly whether *any* feeling — emotion, hedonic tone, hedonic
  bodily feeling or urge — was present. I also found no published reply by Barrett, Russell or
  Watson to the DES 26% figure (absence of a find, not proof of absence).
- **Standing methodological objections on each side.** DES side: checklists and adjective ratings
  create or retrospectively construct feeling at the probe, and questionnaires overstate (79% vs
  18% in the same people). Other side (stated by DES authors themselves): DES cannot rule out
  faint feeling; and under core-affect theory background affect is by hypothesis unattended.
  Schwitzgebel's published scepticism about introspective reports of emotion (Hurlburt &
  Schwitzgebel 2007, 2011) is cited by the DES authors; not read here.

**Mind-blanking and "pure awareness".** Search snippets put mind-blanking at roughly 5–20% of
probes in the literature (**SNIP**); these are reports of no content at all, and I found no
valence breakdown. Gamma & Metzinger (2021, *PLoS ONE*, MPE-92M, 1,403 usable meditator
responses) was located but its affect items were not extracted (**ABS/SNIP**).

## 6. Bodily feeling and urges (for the broad definition)

- **DES Sensory awareness** (thematic attention to a sensory quality, inner or outer; includes
  bodily sensations such as cold on the cheek, and non-hedonic ones such as the redness of a can):
  22% (2008); 33.0%, 30.3%, 27.3% (2022); 65.6% and 58.8% (2015, n = 5). Not split into hedonic vs
  non-hedonic or bodily vs external, and overlap with Feeling is not reported.
- **Arithmetic bound (mine, not the authors').** In Heavey & Hurlburt 2008, Feeling 26% + Sensory
  awareness 22% = at most 48% of samples with either, so at least ~52% had neither; in Hurlburt
  2022 the corresponding floor is ~49–61%. This overcounts the broad "feeling" class on one side
  (sensory awareness includes non-hedonic sensing) and may undercount it on the other (hunger,
  discomfort or urges that were present but not thematic, or coded under one of the ≤3%
  categories). It is a bound, not a measurement.
- **Urges.** Hofmann, Baumeister, Förster & Vohs (2012), Everyday temptations, *JPSP 102*,
  1318–1335 / Hofmann, Vohs & Baumeister (2012), *Psychological Science 23*, 582–588: 205 adults,
  7 signals/day for 7 days, 7,827 desire reports; asked whether they were experiencing a desire
  "now or in the preceding 30 minutes"; a desire was reported on "about half" of signals (**2ND**,
  BPS Research Digest summary; papers themselves blocked). A 30-minute window with a direct
  yes/no prompt; not a single-moment measure.
- **Not found:** any moment-sampling study reporting how often a hedonically toned bodily feeling
  (pain, discomfort, hunger, comfort) is present in experience in healthy adults.

## 7. Things I could not verify

- Sikka 2014/2017/2018 numbers are from the author's thesis table, not the journal articles.
- Merritt et al. 1994 percentage; Sikka et al. 2021 percentages; Killingsworth & Gilbert
  percentages; Hall/Van de Castle percentages; Foulkes/Strauch & Meier — snippets only.
- Fordyce / Boehnke & Schwartz / Lee et al. "neutral X% of the time" and Mesquita & Karasawa —
  secondary citation only; methods not checked.
- Hofmann et al. exact proportion of signals with a desire.
- Russell's "always in some state of core affect" wording — snippet; the p. 148 quotation is
  verbatim via Heavey & Hurlburt 2008.
- No ESM dataset statistic for exactly-neutral valence ratings was found.
- All DES frequency data come from one research group.
