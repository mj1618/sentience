## 2. Explicit negative results

- Honeybees do not shift preference to morphine after pinching or amputation [14f].
- Morphine in shore crabs causes general unresponsiveness, not analgesia [17c].
- Crayfish and shrimps show no nociceptive response to extreme pH, cold or capsaicin, only to heat [18b][18c].
- Frogs and goldfish lacked emotional fever in Cabanac's work [25b]; the fish negative was later overturned in zebrafish [23b].
- Both formal reviews found **no taxon clearly failing a criterion**; gaps are "invariably" lack of positive evidence [1][2]. No decapod has been shown to value an analgesic [1].

## 3. Formal frameworks

**Birch et al. 2021 (LSE)** [1]. Eight criteria: (1) nociceptors; (2) integrative brain regions; (3) connections between them; (4) analgesic-affected responses; (5) motivational trade-offs; (6) flexible self-protection; (7) associative learning beyond habituation; (8) valuing analgesics. Met with high confidence: 7–8 very strong, 5–6 strong, 3–4 substantial. Octopods very strong (1,2,3,4,6,7,8); other coleoids substantial (1,2,3,7); true crabs strong (1,2,4,6,7); anomurans substantial (1,2,6); astacids substantial (1,2,4).

**Gibbons et al. 2022** [2]. Same criteria, six insect orders. Adult Diptera and Blattodea 6 criteria ("strong"); adult Hymenoptera, Lepidoptera, Orthoptera 3–4 ("substantial"); Coleoptera lower; "no good evidence that any insects failed a criterion"; large gaps for juveniles.

**Ginsburg & Jablonka** [3][4]. UAL hallmarks: compound stimuli, novel stimuli, second-order conditioning, trace conditioning, flexibly rewritable value. Claimed distribution: "most vertebrates, some arthropods, and coleoid cephalopods" [4] (their book: all vertebrates, many arthropods, some cephalopods [3]). Three independent origins; the vertebrate one "no later than" the Cambrian, and they argue UAL drove the Cambrian explosion [3][4]. Conjectured absent in gastropods (*Aplysia*), annelids and nematodes, with the admission that these groups "have not been sufficiently studied" and compound-stimulus tests are missing [4]. The *C. elegans* trace and second-order results [10a][10b] sit awkwardly with that conjecture.

## 4. Quantitative comparative studies

- **Sayol et al. 2016** [5]: 1,217 bird species. Relative brain size is larger where vegetation productivity and snow cover vary more; environmental variables explain about 4% of residual brain-size variance overall (19% in Piciformes, 44% in Strigiformes). Residents in seasonal regions have larger brains; migrants smaller. Diet generalists have larger brains independently. Direction of causation unresolved.
- **Sol et al. 2005** [31]: larger-brained birds establish better in novel environments, via innovation propensity.
- **Cognitive buffer / longevity**: relative brain size correlates with longevity in mammals [32] and birds [6]; a 339-species bird analysis supports a direct brain-to-lifespan path [6].
- **Bees** [34]: 86 specialist versus 154 generalist wild bees; diet breadth did not predict associative learning accuracy; specialists simply stopped participating, so the assay is biased toward generalists.
- **Cephalopods** [20c]: Amodio et al. argue intelligence evolved alongside fast life histories, driven by shell loss, predation and challenging foraging niches — an explicit exception to the long-life pattern.

Caveat: these measure brain size, innovation or learning speed, not valence markers.

## 5. Does the record fit the lifetime-novelty prediction?

**Fits**
- Rat, corvid, chicken: long-lived generalists, positive on most tested markers.
- Octopus, cuttlefish (niche axis only): generalist predators, strongest invertebrate evidence.
- Hermit and shore crabs: multi-year generalist scavengers with trade-offs or discrimination learning.
- Bees (niche axis): unpredictable floral resources, richest insect marker set.
- Birds quantitatively: environmental variability and diet breadth both predict brain size [5].
- Bivalves, jellyfish, leech, *Aplysia* fit only weakly: narrow niches, few markers, mostly because nobody looked.

**Counterexamples: short-lived or specialist, yet marker-positive**
- *Drosophila*: about two months, yet second-order conditioning, anxiolytic-sensitive anxiety-like state, play-like behaviour, and Gibbons's "strong" grade.
- Bee workers: weeks-long lives with trade-offs, judgement bias and play.
- Octopus and cuttlefish: 1–2 years, semelparous. Lifespan fails as a predictor here [20c].
- *C. elegans*: 2–3 weeks, 302 neurons, yet hunger-graded trade-off, trace and second-order conditioning.
- Cleaner wrasse: dietary specialist passing the mark test (but socially variable client interactions).
- *Portia*: prey specialist with flexible trial-and-error tactics (sentience markers untested).
- Crayfish: field lifespans of months, anxiety-like state.

**Counterexamples: long-lived or generalist, yet marker-negative**
- Frogs are the only candidate: generalist, multi-year, no emotional fever or tachycardia [25b] — one old paradigm; the matching fish negative was later reversed.
- No long-lived generalist has a replicated negative. Long-lived bivalves are untested, not negative.

**How much is untested**
- Roughly half of the 132 taxon-by-marker cells (about 66) are U. Devaluation is U in 21 of 22 taxa; play in 13; trade-offs in 13.
- Explicit negatives number about five; two concern opioid pharmacology.
- Testing effort follows lab-model status, so marker counts are confounded with attention [1].

**Reading.** The "long life" half of the prediction is not supported: flies, bees, nematodes and cephalopods are short-lived and positive. The "variable niche" half survives better, with quantitative support in birds, though the bee study shows generalist advantage can be an assay artefact. The record cannot distinguish "reflexes win in stable niches" from "stable-niche animals were never tested".
