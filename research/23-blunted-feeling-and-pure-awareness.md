# 23 — Blunted feeling and "pure awareness": evidence brief for Scenario 30 (Predictions 3 and 4)

Gathered 2026-10-04. Evidence only; no scoring. Coding rule quoted from the scenario file:
"I feel nothing"/"everything is flat" alone = normal world minus emotion; "thinned" requires an
altered percept, presence or reality. Depersonalisation/derealisation disorder (DPD) is excluded
from any count; its co-occurrence figures are given in a separately labelled section.

**Access key used throughout**

- **[FULL]** I read the full text (or the full supplement/dataset) myself.
- **[PARTIAL]** I read part of the text, or a machine summary of the full text.
- **[ABSTRACT]** abstract only.
- **[SNIPPET]** search-engine snippet or secondary description only. Treat as unverified.

Things I looked for and could not get are listed in section 5.

## 1. Prediction 3 — conditions where feeling is globally blunted

### 1.1 Antidepressant (SSRI) emotional blunting

**(a) Price J, Cole V, Goodwin GM (2009).** Emotional side-effects of selective serotonin reuptake
inhibitors: qualitative study. *Br J Psychiatry* 195:211–217.
https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-british-journal-of-psychiatry/article/emotional-sideeffects-of-selective-serotonin-reuptakeinhibitors-qualitative-study/88C72E9EA0961CDE777C2FDCDBCE1CA9
Online supplement of quotations:
https://static.cambridge.org/content/id/urn:cambridge.org:id:article:S0007125000250125/resource/name/S0007125000250125sup001.pdf

- Access: supplement **[FULL]** (all 4 pages read); main article **[PARTIAL]** (machine summary).
- Sample: 38 interviewees (19 via poster, 18 via GP mailing, 1 via psychiatrists), semi-structured
  individual interviews; one group interview; 11 validation interviews; 272 web-forum posts.
  Participants were recruited *because* they reported emotional side-effects (self-selected).
- How asked: semi-structured interview about emotional effects of the drug. Quotations in the
  supplement were chosen by the authors to illustrate eight themes, so they are not a random
  sample of what was said. I did not see the interview topic guide.
- Themes: (1) general effects on all emotions; (2) reduction of positive emotions; (3) reduction
  of negative emotions; (4) emotional detachment; (5) just not caring; (6) changed personality;
  (7) effects on everyday life; (8) "it's because of my pills!".
- The supplement quotes 33 distinct participants (my count of participant numbers).

Verbatim quotes that mention unreality, an altered world, or an altered sense of presence
(listed so the adjudicator can apply the coding rule; I have not coded them):

- P22 (sertraline; cannabis 2 nights/week; BDI 12): "Almost as though I was watching life happening
  rather than being a part of it. I didn't feel connected, I felt like I was an observer of what
  was going on around me and not being a part of it and it seemed unreal."
- P22 again (group interview): "And I'd feel unreal, like I was looking at the world through a
  sheet of glass or something, and I just didn't feel like I could communicate with people very
  well and I didn't want to."
- P28 (fluoxetine, on a doubled dose): "then I felt really detached, it felt like I was sitting
  behind myself all the time so it wasn't actually me talking, I was watching me talk to somebody
  else".
- P25 (validation interview, commenting on the questionnaire): "try and explain why you're not
  feeling, why things are different, and the world appears different and your relationships feel
  different."
- P4: "it's like being in limbo, you feel that you're going through your life and doing things
  ... but almost the things happening in the world aren't having an effect on you."
- P12: "I always felt slightly removed from everything ... things didn't really matter somehow."
- P3: "Now it's like I can see the emotion there like almost separate from me."
- P15: "It just feels like it's not real, what's being shown on the outside is not real, it's not
  the real me, it's just the chemical me" (about the self/authenticity of mood, not the world).

Verbatim quotes of the "flat / feel nothing" kind with no mention of percept or reality (examples;
most quoted participants are of this kind):

- P27: "there's no up, there's no down, it's sort of yeah flat ... everything is like flatlined."
- P30: "it was like a blank slate, I just didn't feel anything, I didn't feel anything at all and I
  wasn't worried about anything ... just weird."
- P15: "I'm on this constant emotional plain of kind of blank, blank – not happiness but ok-ness".
- P1: "everything that you do, the experience, is distorted away from the emotional end of the
  spectrum and towards the non-emotional or what I call the cognitive end."
- P35: "sort of empty inside, I just function ... I just feel like I am a robot sweeping the floor."
- P34: "I'd be perfectly aware that I was in a situation or doing something that you know should
  make me happy but it would just have no real effect."

A quote bearing on perception-plus-feeling (ambiguous under the rule):

- P1: "if you are out in the fresh air and you see a particularly amazing scenery ... that too can
  move you very deeply. Now I can remember that those experiences were part of my repertoire
  before being medicated, but they aren't now." (the scene is seen; being moved is missing)
- P18: "I used to have this really strong and vivid images and pictures that I painted, but the
  medication killed the whole lot" (imagery/creativity, not perception).

**(b) Oxford Questionnaire on the Emotional Side-effects of Antidepressants (OQESA), now Oxford
Depression Questionnaire (ODQ).** Price J, Cole V, Doll H, Goodwin GM (2012). *J Affect Disord*
140:66–74. Sample copy:
https://innovation.ox.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2014/09/Final_ODQ_English_UK_SAMPLE-1.pdf

- Access: sample questionnaire **[FULL]** (all 26 items read); the 2012 validation paper not read.
- 26 items, 5-point agree/disagree, "during the past week". Only **one** item concerns the world
  rather than emotion: item 8, "I feel 'spaced out' and distant from the world around me."
  There is no item on unreality, altered perception, or things looking flat/lifeless.
  Emotion-dulling items include item 1 "All my emotions, both 'pleasant' and 'unpleasant', are
  'toned down'", item 9 "My emotions lack intensity", item 17 "My emotions are numbed / dulled /
  flattened compared to before".
- **Item-level endorsement rates for item 8 versus items 1/9/17: not found in anything I could
  read.** Only subscale scores are published in the sources I accessed.

**(c) Goodwin GM, Price J, De Bodinat C, Laredo J (2017).** Emotional blunting with antidepressant
treatments: a survey among depressed patients. *J Affect Disord* 221:31–35.
Author manuscript: https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:8a7067a3-7b21-481f-ac80-32f1929d3ff7

- Access: author manuscript **[FULL]** including tables.
- Sample: internet panels in Canada, USA, UK; 669 depressed patients on one antidepressant, 150
  recovered controls off medication.
- Screening question (exact): "To what extent have you been experiencing emotional effects of your
  antidepressant?" qualified by: "emotional effects vary, but may include, for example, feeling
  emotionally 'numbed' or 'blunted' in some way; lacking positive emotions or negative emotions;
  feeling detached from the world around you; or 'just not caring' about things that you used to
  care about." Note the probe itself offers "detached from the world around you" as an example.
- Result: 46% (310/669) answered mildly/moderately/severely. Of these, 37% rated the blunting
  negatively, 38% positively, 25% neutral.
- Subscale means (Table 3; mean ± SD):

  | | Blunted, HAD-D 8–12 (n=170) | Blunted, HAD-D ≤7 (n=140) | Recovered controls (n=150) |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | General reduction | 13.2 ± 3.9 | 11.2 ± 4.2 | 6.8 ± 4.6 |
  | Emotional detachment | 8.5 ± 5.7 | 5.6 ± 5.1 | 5.0 ± 4.8 |
  | Not caring | 12 ± 3.5 | 7.7 ± 4.3 | 6.2 ± 4.0 |
  | Positive reduction | 15.6 ± 3.4 | 10.6 ± 4.9 | 7.8 ± 5.4 |

  Authors: "The 'General reduction' ... is most clearly associated with current antidepressant
  monotherapy. Emotional detachment is largely accounted for by differences in HAD-D depression
  scores." Total score correlated with HAD-Depression r = 0.521.
- The "emotional detachment" subscale is mostly detachment *from other people* (items on sympathy
  and others' feelings); item 8 is the only world-directed item, and which subscale it loads on
  I could not confirm. So the subscale is not a clean measure of unreality.

### 1.2 Auto-activation deficit / athymhormia / "psychic akinesia" (bilateral basal ganglia, frontal-medial lesions)

**Laplane D, Dubois B (2001).** Auto-activation deficit: a basal ganglia related syndrome.
*Mov Disord* 16:810–814. https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/2001-laplane.pdf — **[FULL]**

- A review, not a counted series; draws on case reports by Laplane (1981, 1982, 1984, 1988, 1989),
  Ali-Cherif (1984), Trillet (1990), Danel (1991), Bogousslavsky (1991), Damasio & Van Hoesen (1983).
  No total patient number is given.
- How asked: patients are questioned about what is in their mind when left alone. The authors note
  some patients "were not asked more precise questions about their mental activity".
- First-person content: "Their mind is 'empty, a total blank,' they say. In the most typical cases,
  they have no thoughts and no projections in the future." "reported in almost the same terms by
  numerous patients." "Not all patients with AAD experience this surprising mental vacuum".
- Affect: "Affect usually appears flattened and emotional responses are blunted." "They are not
  anxious, they express neither fear nor desire". "they can generally react appropriately to good or
  bad news. But the reaction is short-lived and they rapidly return to their usual 'neutral' state.
  ... they appear to have no feelings when left alone." One patient "was not bored by his condition,
  but it surprised him."
- Perception/reality: **no description of altered perception, unreality or derealisation anywhere
  in the paper.** Cognition is described as normal under stimulation (normal WAIS and Raven's).
  The paper does not say perception was asked about.
- Authors' own gloss (relevant to Prediction 4 as well): "They describe a mental state ... almost
  unimaginable to a normal conscious human being: conscious awareness without any content, i.e.,
  consciousness per se."
- Mixed cases: Danel et al. reported three cases with major depression plus AAD; the authors say it
  is "possible to experience concurrently both mental pain or anhedonia and an empty mind."
- Akinetic mutism recovery (Damasio & Van Hoesen 1983, as reported by Laplane & Dubois): a woman
  with bilateral anterior cingulate/SMA lesions "explained that she had nothing to say during this
  phase and that she had an 'empty mind.'" Three further patients after SMA ablation "declared
  that if they remained quasi-mute, it was because they had nothing to say." Original chapter not
  read.

**Kaphan E et al. (2014).** *Arch Clin Neuropsychol* 29:715–723. Single case, 25-year follow-up;
"reported having feelings of mental emptiness"; "never expressed any affects"; no alteration of
reality perception documented. **[PARTIAL]** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4263928

**Leu-Semenescu S et al. (2013).** *Brain* 136:3076–3084. 13 AAD patients with daytime mental
blank; 4 of 13 reported dreams on REM awakening (short, mundane). **[SNIPPET]** (press coverage).

Levy R, Dubois B (2006) *Cereb Cortex* 16:916–928: not read.

### 1.3 PTSD emotional numbing

- Two competing accounts are described in the literature: numbing as a general dampening akin to
  dissociation (Foa) versus a selective deficit in positive emotion with negative emotion intact or
  heightened (Litz). Litz et al. found hyperarousal symptoms the strongest predictor of numbing in
  two Vietnam-veteran samples. "More recent experimental studies suggest that emotional numbing
  primarily reflects diminished positive affect and a reduced tendency to express emotion
  behaviorally." **[SNIPPET]** (Litz & Gray 2002, *Aust N Z J Psychiatry* 36:198–204; Feeny et al.
  2000, *J Trauma Stress* 13:489–498.) So PTSD numbing may not be *global* blunting.
- Dissociative subtype (depersonalisation or derealisation in addition to PTSD): pooled 38.1% of
  people with PTSD across all samples, 22.8% in latent-class samples (systematic review/meta-
  analysis, UEA repository); "roughly 15–30%" in another summary. **[SNIPPET]**
  https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/id/eprint/85780 . Numbing is a core PTSD criterion, so by these
  figures most people with PTSD numbing do not meet the derealisation/depersonalisation subtype.
- **I found no study giving first-person descriptions of PTSD numbing with an explicit check of
  whether perception/reality was altered, and no direct numbing-by-derealisation correlation
  coefficient.** Qualitative veteran studies (e.g. Ray & Vanstone 2009) surfaced only as snippets
  about family relationships.

### 1.4 Schizophrenia: flat affect

**Kring AM, Neale JM (1996).** *J Abnorm Psychol* 105:249–257.
https://esilab.berkeley.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/12/Kring-Neale-1996.pdf — **[ABSTRACT]** (first page read)

- Patients "were less facially expressive than controls during the emotional films and reported
  experiencing as much positive and negative emotion", with greater skin-conductance reactivity.
- Bearing: flat affect in schizophrenia is defined by reduced *expression*; experienced emotion is
  reported as intact. It is therefore weak evidence about blunted *feeling* either way.
  Sample sizes not extracted.

### 1.5 Severe depression: "feeling of no feeling"

- No primary study with counts was obtained. Secondary descriptions say anhedonia in depression is
  described by patients as "missing out", "being numb", and (one qualitative adolescent study,
  Watson et al. 2020, PubMed 31270605) loss of connection and questioning of self. **[SNIPPET]**
- Ratcliffe M (2015), *Experiences of Depression* (OUP): based on a free-text questionnaire run via
  the charity SANE; argues depression alters "existential feeling", a "sense of reality and
  belonging", with people speaking of "'feelings' of unreality ... strangeness, unfamiliarity".
  **[SNIPPET]**; no counts seen.
- Depersonalisation/derealisation symptoms in unipolar depression: up to 60% in clinical samples
  (Hunter, Sierra & David 2004 systematic review, PubMed 15022041). **[SNIPPET]**
- In Goodwin 2017 (above) blunting scores correlate with depression severity (r = 0.521), so
  drug blunting and depressive numbing are not separable in those data.

### 1.6 Reduced bodily feedback: spinal cord injury, pure autonomic failure

- **Hohmann GW (1966).** *Psychophysiology* 3:143–156. 26 men with cord lesions, structured
  interview comparing feelings before and after injury; decreases in experienced anger, sexual
  excitement and fear, larger with higher lesions; increased "sentimentality". **[SNIPPET]** The
  well-known quotes (anger as "a mental kind of anger", "cold") could not be verified: the PDF
  host returned 404. I saw nothing about altered perception or unreality, but did not read the paper.
- **Chwalisz K, Diener E, Gallagher D (1988).** *J Pers Soc Psychol* 54:820–828. No difference
  in emotional experience between spinal-cord-injured and control groups. **[SNIPPET]**
- Bermond et al. (1991): cord lesions "do not result in an overall reduction of emotional
  excitability". **[SNIPPET]**
- **Heims HC, Critchley HD, Dolan R, Mathias CJ, Cipolotti L (2004).** *Neuropsychologia*
  42:1979–1988. Pure autonomic failure patients unimpaired on gambling task, emotional face
  recognition, theory of mind and social cognition; worse only on an emotional attribution test.
  **[SNIPPET]** Nothing on perception/reality seen.
- Bearing: after the replications, these conditions do not clearly produce global blunting at all.

### 1.7 Drugs other than antidepressants

- **Acetaminophen:** Durso GRO, Luttrell A, Way BM (2015). *Psychol Sci* 26:750–758. Two
  experiments; drug group rated unpleasant pictures less negatively, pleasant ones less positively,
  and both as less emotionally arousing; "nonevaluative ratings (extent of color saturation in each
  image; Study 2) were not affected by drug condition." **[ABSTRACT]** A small, picture-rating
  blunting with an explicit perceptual control that did not move. Participants were not asked
  about reality or presence.
- **Opioids, benzodiazepines:** only lay and secondary descriptions found ("numb ... no highs, no
  lows, just flat"). **[SNIPPET]** No systematic first-person data on perception.
- **Propranolol:** studies found concern emotional memory, not ongoing feeling. **[SNIPPET]**
- **Ketamine:** not gathered. Its acute effect is dissociative (derealisation is the drug's
  hallmark) rather than selective blunting of feeling, so it would not isolate the question.

### 1.8 Alexithymia

Not gathered beyond definitions. Alexithymia is difficulty identifying and describing feelings,
not established absence of feeling, so its bearing is unclear. No data on perception found.

### 1.9 Cotard's syndrome

**Berrios GE, Luque R (1995).** *Acta Psychiatr Scand* 91:185–188. 100 literature cases:
depression 89%, nihilistic delusions about the body 86% and about existence 69%, anxiety 65%,
guilt 63%. **[SNIPPET]** High anxiety and guilt rates mean most Cotard cases are not affect-free;
the syndrome is a delusion of non-existence rather than a documented global loss of feeling.
First-person descriptions not obtained.

### 1.10 Depersonalisation/derealisation — excluded from the count; co-occurrence only

- **Baker D et al. (2003).** *Br J Psychiatry* 182:428–433. 204 DPD cases: both depersonalisation
  and derealisation 73%; depersonalisation only 21%; derealisation only 6%. Depersonalisation
  "present" in 96%, "intense" 66%; derealisation "present" 80%, "intense" 49%. Assessment: PSE
  interview (n=124), telephone (n=22), questionnaires (n=58). Patient quote: "I feel nothing —
  never have. When my children were born — nothing." No separate percentage for emotional numbing.
  **[PARTIAL]** https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/6454C9E73A89BF69D09EB5CE986B3F01/core-reader
- **Sierra M, Baker D, Medford N, David AS (2005).** *Psychol Med* 35:1523–1532. Factor analysis of
  the Cambridge Depersonalisation Scale in 138 DPD patients: four factors, 'Anomalous Body
  Experience', 'Emotional Numbing', 'Anomalous Subjective Recall', 'Alienation from Surroundings'
  (73.3% of variance). Emotional numbing and derealisation load on *separate* factors;
  inter-factor correlations not obtained. **[ABSTRACT]**
- Michal M et al. (2016). *BMC Psychiatry* 16:203. 223 DPD patients; 84.8% comorbid depressive
  disorder; no numbing-specific figures. **[PARTIAL]**
- Reading: within DPD, most patients have both numbing-type and unreality-type symptoms (73% have
  both DP and DR), yet the two are statistically separable dimensions. This is circular for
  Prediction 3 (the diagnosis requires unreality) and is reported for information only.

### 1.11 Reported separately, unscored: anhedonia and pain asymbolia

- **Anhedonia:** see 1.5. Qualitative descriptions mix loss of pleasure with "being numb" and
  sometimes derealisation language; no counts obtained.
- **Pain asymbolia:** Berthier M, Starkstein S, Leiguarda R (1988). *Ann Neurol* 24:41–49. Six
  patients, insular damage in all; no primary sensory deficit; "lack of withdrawal and absent or
  inadequate emotional responses to painful stimuli ... as well as to threatening gestures".
  Patients recognise pain but are not bothered. **[SNIPPET]** A selective loss of one feeling with
  sensation intact; no reports of general unreality seen, and none sought in the paper so far as
  I can tell.

### 1.12 Notes that cut across Prediction 3

- In no source outside DPD did I find the question "does the world look or feel different/unreal?"
  asked systematically of people with blunted feeling. Blunting instruments ask about emotions;
  the single world-directed ODQ item has no published item-level rate that I could find.
- Where unreality language appears in the SSRI interviews it comes from a minority of quoted
  participants (see 1.1a), in a sample selected for emotional side-effects, with depression and,
  in one case, cannabis use as possible alternative sources.
- In auto-activation deficit, the most extreme documented blunting, the reports are of an empty
  mind and no feelings, with no mention of altered perception; but perception was not reported as
  having been probed, and thought is lost along with feeling.

## 2. Prediction 4 (scored part) — contentless "pure awareness" with neither feeling nor thought

### 2.1 Metzinger's Minimal Phenomenal Experience (MPE) project

**Gamma A, Metzinger T (2021).** The Minimal Phenomenal Experience questionnaire (MPE-92M).
*PLoS ONE* 16(7):e0253694. https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0253694
Data: https://osf.io/cbhdv/ (file `MPE92M_data_set_quesionnaires_documentation.zip`; the DOI link
printed in the paper, osf.io/gb76x, now returns 410).

- Access: paper **[FULL]**; English questionnaire **[PARTIAL]** (instructions read); public
  dataset **[FULL]** — I downloaded it and computed the item statistics below myself.
- Sample: 3,627 submissions, 1,403 usable (>85 of 92 items complete). Median age 52; 77.3% daily
  meditators; median 10 years' practice; Vipassana 43.9%, Zen 34.9%.
- How asked: participants who said they had ever had an experience of "pure awareness" were told to
  pick "one single, particular experience" and rate 92 items on 0–100 visual analogue scales
  ("No" to "Very much so"/"Very many"). Retrospective, sometimes by years. The instructions state
  "it does not matter whether or not this state was accompanied by any other kinds of experiential
  qualities or contents, i.e., whether or not you also had perceptions, emotions, or thoughts at
  the same time." So the sample is *not* restricted to contentless episodes.
- Factor structure: 12 factors, 44% of variance: Time, Effort and Desire; **Peace, Bliss and
  Silence**; Self-Knowledge, Autonomous Cognizance and Insight; Wakeful Presence; Pure Awareness in
  Dream and Sleep; Luminosity; **Thoughts and Feelings**; Emptiness and Non-egoic Self-awareness;
  Sensory Perception in Body and Space; Touching World and Self; Mental Agency; Witness
  Consciousness. Factor 2 items: relaxed (loading 0.76), at peace (0.72), at ease (0.72), whole
  (0.54), "subtle but clearly positive mood, e.g. like an 'invisible smile'" (0.47),
  "blissfulness" (0.45), deep silence, "natural state", "non-tactile and non-emotional
  'gentleness'", pure being, positive mood (0.32). "Did you have emotions?" loads on Factor 7 with
  thoughts, images, memories (0.44), not on Factor 2.
- Authors' statements: "The highest average ratings were given to items related to wellbeing and
  relaxation; the overall lowest rating concerned the presence of pain." "A preliminary qualitative
  analysis of 841 phenomenological reports indicates that MPE as such is not an emotional state,
  but that it can certainly trigger a whole spectrum of mostly positive affective states like joy,
  existential relief, gratitude, unpersonal love, awe and wonder". "MPE can sometimes co-exist with
  a mostly subtle but clearly noticeable form of bliss". The paper gives no count of affect-free
  reports.

**My own item-level tabulation of the public data (N = 1,403; not in the paper):**

| Item (0–100) | Median | Rated 0 | Rated ≤10 | Rated ≥50 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 7 "Did you have emotions?" | 26 | 17.6% | 33.0% | 38.8% |
| 8 "Was your mood positive?" | 86 | 1.2% | 1.6% | 95.5% |
| 64 "quality of 'blissfulness'?" | 85 | 4.2% | 6.3% | 86.7% |
| 65 "subtle but clearly positive mood ... 'invisible smile'?" | 86 | 4.2% | 6.3% | 88.7% |
| 69 "Did you feel at peace?" | 98 | 0.7% | 0.8% | 97.7% |
| 70 "Did you feel at ease?" | 99 | 0.6% | 0.7% | 97.7% |
| 71 "Did you feel relaxed?" | 95 | 0.4% | 0.7% | 96.7% |
| 90 "sense of awe or wonder?" | 79 | 8.7% | 13.6% | 76.4% |
| 17 "Was there pain?" | 0 | 52.1% | 77.7% | 6.7% |

| 3 "Did you have thoughts?" | 20 | 14.9% | 36.7% | 24.7% |

- Reports with **no feeling on every affect item** (complete cases):
  - Narrow set (emotions, positive mood, bliss, invisible smile, awe) all ≤10: **5 of 1,371
    (0.36%)**; all exactly 0: 3 (0.22%); all ≤25: 7 (0.51%).
  - Broad set (narrow + peace, ease, relaxed, whole, pain, desires) all ≤10: **2 of 1,366 (0.15%)**.
  - Neither thought nor feeling (thought items 3–6, 26, 27 all ≤10 *and* affect all ≤10): narrow 5
    of 1,349 (0.37%); broad 2 of 1,344 (0.15%).
- Reading the "emotions" item alone gives a different picture: 33.0% rated emotions ≤10. Among
  those 459 people, median positive mood was 87, peace 100, bliss 85; 4.2% rated positive mood ≤10
  and 10.9% rated bliss ≤10. That is, a third deny "emotions" while most of them rate peace, ease
  and positive mood near ceiling.
- Strictest contentless subset I could define (thoughts ≤10, perceptions ≤10, emotions ≤10, and
  "awareness itself the only content" ≥75): n = 162 (11.5%). In it: peace median 100 (1 person
  ≤10), relaxed median 100 (1 ≤10), positive mood median 92 (7.5% ≤10), bliss median 90 (11.7%
  ≤10), invisible smile median 86 (16.0% ≤10), awe median 70 (30.2% ≤10), pain ≤10 in 98.1%.
  Exactly 1 of the 162 rated all eight positive-tone items below 50.
- Experiences rated as occurring in dreamless deep sleep (item 83 ≥50; n = 82): peace median 96,
  positive mood median 80, bliss median 80; emotions ≤10 in 30.5%.
- Caveats that apply to these numbers: retrospective single-episode ratings; participants were told
  to choose an episode that was "particularly salient"; the authors acknowledge some chose
  "particularly impressive or personally meaningful experiences"; the items are leading yes-type
  questions on unipolar scales ("Did you feel at peace?"), with no neutral-probe item and no
  "neither pleasant nor unpleasant" option; meditative traditions teach that the state is
  peaceful/blissful. Whether "at peace/at ease/relaxed" is a hedonic feeling or the absence of
  disturbance is an interpretive question the data cannot settle. My threshold choices (≤10, ≤25)
  are arbitrary; the script is at

### 2.2 Contentless states as described by meditation experts

**Woods TJ, Windt JM, Carter O (2022).** Evidence synthesis indicates contentless experiences in
meditation are neither truly contentless nor identical. *Phenomenol Cogn Sci*.
https://doi.org/10.1007/s11097-022-09811-z (read via University of Melbourne repository) — **[PARTIAL]**
(searched full text for affect passages)

- Method: 135 expert texts from Shamatha, Transcendental Meditation and Stillness Meditation; 65
  features of the goal-states extracted.
- On feeling: "in all three practices the experts repeatedly state that there are no feelings in
  the goal-states. In other places, however, they identify features of the goal-states that could
  be construed as feelings. The most obvious examples are the experiences of bliss, goodness, calm,
  ease and peacefulness."
- Features reported/implied in all three practices: "Calm, ease, peacefulness, absence of
  disturbance"; "Relaxation"; "No discomfort"; "No feeling of anxiety"; "Bliss, joy, happiness,
  goodness, sense of wellbeing" (Stillness Meditation does not use the term bliss).
- Dissent inside TM: Shear "sometimes treats [bliss] as part of the TM goal-state ... and other
  times he expressly states that it is not"; he holds the state lacks "all abstract content too",
  using bliss as an example, and that "the meditator can report that they were conscious and awake,
  but can say almost nothing more." Travis "tends not to refer to bliss."
- Wallace on Shamatha: bliss is "subtle", "subdued", a "joy of serenity", arising when the mind is
  no longer "pummeled to death with afflictions".
- Authors' conclusion: "numerous forms of content are present in the experiences, including
  wakefulness, naturalness, calm, bliss/joy, and freedom."
- These are expert/teacher texts, not sampled first-person reports.

### 2.3 Objectless awareness in sleep ("witnessing sleep", dreamless sleep experience)

**Alcaraz-Sánchez A, Demšar E, Campillo-Ferrer T, Torres-Platas SG (2022).** Nothingness is all
there is: an exploration of objectless awareness during sleep. *Front Psychol* 13:901031.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9226678 — **[PARTIAL]** (full text searched for the
emotion section and summary table)

- Sample: 573 survey respondents; 18 selected for micro-phenomenological interviews; 12 analysed.
  Most were already familiar with the idea of such states.
- How asked: micro-phenomenological interview about how one specific episode unfolded; "Emotion"
  was a coding category for mentions of "Presence" or "Absence" of an emotional tone. An "affective
  valence" subcategory was dropped "given its very few mentions".
- Results (text): "Six participants described the state of nothingness as accompanied by good
  sensations such as 'feeling good' or 'refreshed' (P9), 'happy' (P16 and P17), 'glad' (P18), or
  'relaxed' (P12). The other two described the state as 'contentment' (P1) or 'acceptance' (P15),
  which was accompanied by a 'slight sense' of 'disappointment' ... The other three mentioned the
  absence of any feelings or emotions. P2 reported not remembering having had any feelings, whereas
  P8 described how a relaxing and 'narcotic feeling' they previously had ... was lost. P3 described
  their feelings as 'flat,' as lacking emotion. Finally, P13 did not allude to any emotional
  sensations".
- Results (Table 10): "Presence emotions" 7/12; no mention 2/12. The text names 3 with absence and
  1 with no mention, so the paper is slightly inconsistent; on the text, **3 of 12 explicitly
  reported no feeling** in the "nothingness phase" (one of them as not remembering any), 7–8 of 12
  reported mild positive feeling.
- Abstract summary: the phase had "relatively pleasant emotions".
- The companion online survey (Alcaraz-Sánchez 2021, *Dreaming*) could not be accessed (403).

**Windt JM, Nielsen T, Thompson E (2016).** Does consciousness disappear in dreamless sleep?
*Trends Cogn Sci* 20:871–882. Proposes a taxonomy of dreamless sleep experience including
"selfless states" with only a sense of time or bare awareness. Conceptual; not read. **[SNIPPET]**

### 2.4 White dreams and sleep onset

- White dreams (a feeling of having dreamt with no recallable content) occur on roughly 33–39% of
  laboratory serial awakenings and 20–30% of home awakenings (Cohen 1972; Cohen & Wolfe 1973;
  Siclari et al. 2013, 2017, as summarised in *Int J Dream Res*). **[SNIPPET]** The dominant view
  is that they are forgotten dreams; alternatives hold they are low-quality or minimal-content
  experiences (Fazekas, Nemeth & Overgaard 2019; Windt et al. 2016). **No affect data found**:
  nobody appears to ask whether a white dream had a feeling tone.
- Sleep onset: nothing gathered that probes feeling in contentless sleep-onset moments.

### 2.5 Mind-blanking

- **Ward AF, Wegner DM (2013).** Mind-blanking: when the mind goes away. *Front Psychol* 4:650.
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3784796 — **[PARTIAL]**. Seven experiments (n = 23, 27,
  56, 75, 27, 108, 143). Definition given to participants: "not only were you not really thinking
  about the text, you were not thinking about anything at all—your mind was a complete blank."
  Blanking on 14.49% of probes in Experiment 1. **Feeling was not probed.**
- **Mortaheb S, ... Boulakis PA, ... Demertzi A (2022).** *PNAS* 119:e2200511119.
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9564098 — **[PARTIAL]**. 36 participants, 50 probes each
  in the scanner at rest; four response options: "absence" (mind blanking), perception of sensory
  stimuli, stimulus-dependent thoughts, stimulus-independent thoughts. Blanking was the least
  frequent report. **No feeling/affect option or rating.**
- **Kawagoe T, Onoda K, Yoshimura S (2026).** *BMC Psychol*. n = 183; adding a "Forgot" option
  changes what blanking reports mean; cites blanking rates of 3–15% of probes. **[PARTIAL]**
  **No affect probe.**
- Boulakis et al. (2026), Liège Mind-Blanking Scale, and Andrillon, Lutz, Windt & Demertzi (2025,
  *Trends Cogn Sci*): located but not read; I do not know whether the new scale has affect items.
- Bearing: mind-blanking studies establish reports of no thought/no content at 3–18% of probes, but
  none that I read asked about feeling, so they are silent on "no feeling tone".

### 2.6 Cessation (nirodha, nirodha samāpatti)

- **Laukkonen RE, Sacchet MD, Barendregt H, Devaney KJ, Chowdhury A, Slagter HA (2023).** Cessations
  of consciousness in meditation. *Prog Brain Res* 280:61–87.
  https://doi.org/10.1016/bs.pbr.2022.12.007 — **[ABSTRACT]**. Practitioners "report that it is
  possible to undergo a total absence of consciousness during meditation, lasting up to 7 days".
  "Cessations are also associated with the absence of any time experience or tiredness".
  "Emergence ... is said to have profound effects on subsequent cognition and experience (e.g.,
  resulting in a sudden sense of clarity, openness, and possibly insights)."
- Psyche essay on the same work (one main subject, Delson Armstrong) — **[PARTIAL]**: "It's like a
  shutting off of functions. The mind just disconnects completely from all perception and tension,
  and then it's like you're sinking into something." Afterwards meditators "describe a sense of
  clarity and relief, ease and peace." Laukkonen, of his own cessation: "a gap in his conscious
  experience".
  https://psyche.co/ideas/what-happens-to-the-brain-during-consciousness-ending-meditation
- A search summary states Armstrong "reports no presence of any sort of unconditional
  consciousness/knowing, luminosity, Love, or anything else" during the state. **[SNIPPET]**
- Chowdhury A et al. (2023), *Neuropsychologia* (single adept, 37 cessation events): located, not
  readable.
- Bearing: cessation is reported as an *absence of experience*, not as awareness without feeling.
  Feeling tone is reported before (not obtained) and after (peace, relief, clarity).

### 2.7 Auto-activation deficit as a "pure awareness" candidate

See 1.2. Laplane & Dubois describe waking patients with "no thoughts" who "appear to have no
feelings when left alone" and gloss this as "conscious awareness without any content". This is the
one clinical source in this brief that reports both no thought and no feeling in an awake person.
Limits: no counts; reports are retrospective answers to a clinician; feeling was inferred partly
from demeanour ("appear to have no feelings"); patients were "not asked more precise questions".

## 3. Whether this has been tested head-on

I found no study designed to ask whether blunting feeling thins perception/reality, and no
pure-awareness study using a neutral feeling probe at a sampled moment. The nearest are the
Sierra et al. factor analysis (numbing and unreality as separable factors within DPD) and the
Durso acetaminophen colour-saturation control.
