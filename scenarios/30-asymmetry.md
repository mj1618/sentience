# Scenario 30 — Round 15: is there experience without feeling? (the asymmetry test)

**Predictions written and committed before evidence is gathered.** Wording to be checked
for fairness by an independent agent before any evidence is scored.

## The conjecture

The owner's hypothesis: consciousness = sentience + thought, with feeling as the base.
If feeling is the base, the two ways experience could come apart are not symmetrical:

- **feeling without thought** should occur;
- **experience with no feeling at all** (thought or perception, zero feeling) should not.

## Definitions (fixed here)

- **Feeling:** any felt pleasantness or unpleasantness, emotion, mood, bodily feeling with
  a hedonic tone (pain, comfort, hunger, tension), or felt urge. Sensory content with no
  hedonic tone (seeing a grey wall) is *not* feeling.
- **Thought:** inner speech, reasoning, planning, imagery deliberately used, or
  propositional content ("I was thinking that…").
- **A no-feeling experience:** a report of experience in which the person, *asked directly
  whether any feeling in the above sense was present*, says none was; or which a careful
  moment-sampling method classes as lacking any feeling.
- Evidence from **unprompted free reports** (which under-mention feeling) and from
  **emotion checklists** (which invite ticking something) both count only as weak; the
  strongest evidence is from methods that ask neutrally about a single sampled moment.

## Accounts and what each predicts

**A. Feeling-first (owner).** Feeling is the ground of every experience; thought is added.
1. No-feeling experiences are absent or rare under careful probing.
2. Feeling-without-thought experiences are common.
3. Where feeling is flattened (depersonalisation, severe anhedonia, emotional numbing,
   pain asymbolia), people describe experience itself as thinned, unreal or absent, not
   merely "neutral but otherwise normal".
4. Where thought is abolished or minimal (deep absorption, sleep onset, severe cognitive
   impairment), feeling persists.

**B. Cognition-first** (global-workspace and higher-order families). Experience is a matter
of information being available or represented; feeling is one content among others.
1. No-feeling experiences are common (neutral perceiving, abstract thinking).
2. Feeling without any thought-like access is not reportable, so reports of it are rare.
3. Flattened feeling leaves the rest of experience intact; any "unreality" is a separate
   symptom.
4. Loss of thought/access removes reportable feeling too.

**C. Separate-contents view** (no hierarchy; e.g. descriptive experience sampling as
usually reported). Feeling, thinking, sensing are separate kinds of content, each present
in some moments and absent in others.
1. No-feeling experiences common. 2. Feeling-without-thought common. 3. Flattening of
feeling does not thin experience generally. 4. Either can go without the other.

**D. Constant-background-affect view** (core affect). Some pleasant/unpleasant tone is
always present but is not the *base* of experience.
1. As A. 2. As A. 3. As B (flattening changes tone, not the reality of experience).
4. Not committed.

A and D are not separated by prediction 1; only prediction 3 separates them.

## What would count (thresholds fixed here)

Prediction 1, using the best neutral-probe evidence the gatherer can find:
- **against A and D:** no-feeling moments make up more than 20% of sampled experience in
  methods that ask directly and neutrally;
- **for A and D:** under 5%;
- between: undecided.
If the only available evidence comes from methods where "feeling" means "an emotion the
person noticed" (narrower than the definition above), that is to be said, and the result
scored as *undecided with a lean*, not as a verdict.

Prediction 3: for A if most first-person descriptions of flattened feeling describe
experience as unreal/thinned in general; against A if most describe a normal world minus
emotion. The adjudicator decides whether the evidence allows a count.

## What the author already knows (disclosed, so it cannot be hidden)

- From memory, unverified: descriptive experience sampling reports "feelings" in only
  about a quarter of sampled moments. That leans against A, depending on what the method
  counts as feeling.
- From our own data, exploratory: in the Turku dreams, 0 of 113 were rated feeling-free on
  an emotion checklist, which leans for A but is the weak kind of evidence.
- Earlier in this conversation the author told the owner that nobody had tested this
  head-on. The gatherer is asked to check that; if there is an existing debate (e.g.
  whether affect is a constant feature of consciousness), the update will say so.

## A loophole to close now

"There was a feeling but the person did not notice it" would make A unfalsifiable. It is
not available to A in this round: an unnoticed feeling is not counted as feeling.
