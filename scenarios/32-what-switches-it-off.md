# Scenario 32 — Round 17: what switches experience off?

**Predictions written and committed before evidence is gathered.** Wording to be checked
for fairness before any evidence is scored.

## The question

General anaesthetics are chemically unrelated (a noble gas, ethers, steroids, simple
alcohols, ketamine) yet all abolish responsiveness, and they act on organisms from humans
to flies, plants and single cells. What do they have in common, and does that say where
experience comes from? This round also revisits the owner's field/quantum question at the
one place it touches data.

## Three things anaesthesia removes (kept apart throughout)

Responsiveness (does not move or answer), memory (recalls nothing afterwards), and
experience (nothing it was like). A drug can remove the first two and not the third.
Evidence about immobility or unresponsiveness is not evidence about experience unless a
source connects them.

## Accounts and what each predicts

**L. Lipid/membrane.** Anaesthetics act by dissolving in or reorganising cell membranes.
1. Potency tracks oil solubility across all agents, with no exceptions needing a protein.
2. Mirror-image molecules (same solubility) are equally potent.
3. Works on any organism with membranes, including those without nervous systems.
4. Nuclear spin of the anaesthetic atom is irrelevant.

**P. Protein targets, no single one.** Different drugs bind different proteins (ion
channels and receptors); there is no common molecular target.
1. Solubility correlation has exceptions (compounds that should work and do not; a
   size cut-off).
2. Mirror-image molecules can differ in potency.
3. Action on organisms without nervous systems goes through conserved proteins, or is a
   different effect given the same name.
4. Nuclear spin irrelevant.

**Q. Quantum/microtubule.** Anaesthetics act on quantum-sensitive processes inside
neurons, in particular in microtubules (Hameroff–Penrose and related).
1. Potency tracks a physical property tied to the quantum mechanism (e.g. polarisability),
   and binding to microtubule protein matters.
2. Not committed.
3. Works wherever microtubules exist.
4. Nuclear spin of the anaesthetic atom can change potency; drugs that stabilise
   microtubules change sensitivity to anaesthetics.

**N. Network endpoint.** Molecular targets differ; what is common is the end state of the
brain: loss of long-range communication/integration, especially cortex and thalamus.
1–4 as P.
5. One network-level measure tracks loss of *experience* (not just responsiveness) across
   all agents, including ketamine, under which people are unresponsive yet dreaming.
6. No single small site is sufficient; reversal needs restoring network communication.

**B. Brainstem/arousal switch.** Anaesthetics act chiefly by engaging the brain's own
sleep and arousal switches deep in the brainstem and hypothalamus. (Closest to the
feeling-first view, which locates the source of experience in those structures.)
1–4 as P.
5. Cortical measures track the *contents* of experience; presence or absence is set below.
6. A small brainstem site is sufficient: a tiny dose placed there produces anaesthesia;
   stimulating arousal nuclei reverses it while the drug is still present.

## What separates them

- L vs P: predictions 1–2. Q vs all: prediction 4. N vs B: predictions 5–6.
- Cross-kingdom action (3) is offered by L and Q as support; P must explain it.

## What would count

The adjudicator scores each prediction as supported / contradicted / undecided, weighting
replication, pre-registration, sample size, and whether a result concerns experience or
only responsiveness. A single unreplicated study, however striking, scores "undecided
with a lean" at most.

## What the author already believes (disclosed)

From memory, unverified: pure lipid theories were set back by mirror-image and cut-off
findings decades ago, but membrane-based mechanisms have returned in a newer form; a
report exists that xenon isotopes with nuclear spin are weaker anaesthetics in mice, and
the author does not know whether it has been replicated; a report exists that a
microtubule-stabilising drug delayed anaesthesia in rats; microinjection studies have
pointed to a small brainstem area; a brain-complexity measure based on magnetic
stimulation is reported to separate ketamine from other agents. The author expects P+N to
score best on molecular questions, Q to rest on few unreplicated studies, and N vs B to
be undecided.

## No-rescue rules

Q may not appeal to effects too small to measure. P may not rename any unexplained effect
"a different phenomenon" without independent grounds. N and B may not both claim a result
that only shows "the brain changed".
