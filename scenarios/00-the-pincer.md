# Scenario 0 — The pincer: what any answer must survive

Before gaming out individual theories, fix the two facts that squeeze all of them.

## Jaw 1 — We talk about it (the report constraint)

People say "pain feels awful" and write books about experience. Those are physical
events: air moves, fingers press keys. Trace the causes backwards and you reach motor
neurons, then the networks driving them. So either

- **(R1)** sentience is somewhere in that causal chain, or
- **(R2)** it is not, and everything we have ever said about sentience was produced by
  something else and is only accidentally about it.

R2 is self-undermining: if it were true, our belief in sentience would not be evidence
for sentience. So we take R1: **sentience causally influences neurons.**

## Jaw 2 — The physics of neurons is closed to very high precision

Neurons are made of electrons, protons, neutrons, interacting electromagnetically.
The laws for those at body temperature are known and tested to many decimal places
(see `research/02-physics-constraints.md`, `scenarios/02-sentience-as-field.md`).
There is very little room for an extra influence pushing ions around.

## What survives the pincer

Put the jaws together: sentience influences neurons, and nothing unknown influences
neurons at any strength we can find room for. The options:

| # | Option | What it says | Price |
|---|--------|--------------|-------|
| A | **Identity / functionalism** | Sentience *is* some physical or computational process already in the brain. | Must say which process and why that one feels like something (the "hard problem" stays as an explanatory gap, not a causal one). |
| B | **Intrinsic-nature monism** (Russellian) | Physics describes what matter *does*; sentience is what matter *is*. No new forces. | Must explain how micro-level "being" combines into one unified feeling (combination problem). No physics experiment can ever test it; only the combination rule is testable. |
| C | **Existing-field identity** | Sentience is the brain's electromagnetic field (a special case of A). | Makes sharp predictions, some of which look bad (split brains, MRI). |
| D | **New physics** | A new field or a modification of quantum mechanics that acts on neurons. | Must fit through the experimental bounds. Scenario 2 quantifies how small the gap is. |
| E | **Epiphenomenalism** | Sentience is produced by the brain but does nothing. | Fails Jaw 1, *and* cannot explain why good-for-you feels good (Scenario 1, experiment J). |
| F | **Illusionism** | There is no sentience beyond the brain's model that it has some. | Must explain why the model exists and why it is so compelling; many find it denies the datum. |

**First insight of the project.** E is eliminated twice over. D is the only option that
physics experiments can test, and it is the one physics leaves least room for. So the
live contest is A vs B vs F, and *all three agree on the physical story*: there is some
specific organisation of ordinary matter that goes with sentience. The productive
question is therefore not "what extra ingredient is sentience?" but
**"which organisation, and what is the minimal version of it?"**

That is a question we can attack with simulations and with the dissociation evidence
(which brain parts can you lose and still feel?).

## How the owner's hypothesis fits

"Consciousness = sentience + thought, with sentience as the motivator" is a claim about
*which organisation*: the motivational/valence core, not the cortex's thinking machinery.
That places it with the affect-first theories (Panksepp, Damasio, Solms, Merker). It is
compatible with A, B and F, and it is testable by dissociation:

- feeling without thought should exist (and seems to: `research/03`),
- thought without feeling should exist (blindsight, possibly current AI),
- remove the feeling and thought should lose its *drive* rather than its *ability*.
