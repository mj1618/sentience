# Scenario 5 — Two of our predictions meet the data

Sources: `research/05-neutral-experience.md`, `research/06-comparative-data.md`.

## 5a. "Feeling tracks novelty within a lifetime" (from Scenario 1A)

We predicted that a feeling-like signal pays only when the world changes faster than
genes can track, so long-lived generalists should show the signs of feeling and
short-lived specialists should not.

The comparative record across 22 kinds of animal:

- **The lifespan half fails.** Fruit flies (two months), worker bees (weeks) and the
  nematode worm *C. elegans* (2–3 weeks, 302 neurons) all show marker behaviours:
  trade-offs that depend on hunger, learning about learned cues, anxiety-like states
  that respond to anti-anxiety drugs, even play-like behaviour in flies and bees.
  Octopuses live only 1–2 years and have the strongest invertebrate evidence.
- **The variable-niche half survives.** Generalists in changeable habitats score
  highest, and in birds environmental variability and diet breadth predict brain size.
- **No clean negative exists.** No long-lived generalist has a replicated "tested, not
  found". About half of the 132 animal-by-marker cells are untested, and testing effort
  follows brain size and lab popularity.

**Correction to C5.** What matters is unpredictability *relative to the genome*, not
years lived. A fly meets rotting fruit its genes could not have specified.

**The more important lesson.** The standard markers (trade-offs, flexible learning,
drug-sensitive anxiety) are present nearly everywhere anyone has looked, down to a worm
with 302 neurons. Either feeling is nearly universal among animals, or these markers
detect the *valence signal* and not whether it is *felt*. Scenario 1 said as much: the
signal is cheap and useful and should be widespread. The markers cannot draw the line.

Scenario 3 proposes a marker that might: whether an animal's deliberate, goal-directed
behaviour updates only after it *experiences* an outcome in a new bodily state.
**That test has been run in 1 of the 22 taxa (rats).** The single most informative
comparative experiment for our question has barely been tried.

## 5b. Is all experience feeling-or-thought?

The owner's equation has two terms. Plainly seeing a grey wall seems to be neither.
Three ways out, and what the evidence says:

| Option | Claim | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| (a) Faint valence | All seeing carries a trace of good/bad | People can rate anything for pleasantness, but that shows valence can be *extracted*, not that it *makes up* the seeing. Its own proponents concede values "close to zero". |
| (b) Third ingredient | Being awake and perceiving is its own thing | **Best supported.** In depersonalisation, feeling and the sense of reality drop but colour and contrast matching are unchanged (registered report, 2025; strong evidence for no effect). Auto-activation patients have neither thoughts nor feelings yet are awake and aware: "a total blank", "not bored". Deep-meditation reports of contentless awareness describe it as not itself an emotion. |
| (c) Seeing is built from evaluation (Humphrey) | Sensation evolved from an evaluative bodily reaction | Weakest: an evolutionary story plus a contested reading of blindsight. |

**Result.** The equation needs a third term:

> consciousness = **wakeful awareness** (the stage being lit) + **sentience** (felt
> good/bad, the motivating channel) + **thought**

A caveat that keeps the original idea alive: the brain's monitoring of heartbeat and
body state predicts whether a faint image is consciously seen (Park 2014). If
"sentience" is widened from good/bad feeling to *the felt presence of one's own body*,
the third ingredient may fold back into the first. That is a redefinition, not a
finding; flagged as open.

**Consequence for the project.** The mystery is not located only in valence. Whatever
makes a neutral sight experienced is the same puzzle. But valence remains the best
handle, because it is the part with a clear job, clear evolutionary logic, and clear
lesion evidence.
