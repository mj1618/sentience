Update 2 · 3 October 2026

A second look broke much of what we thought we had shown

This round we tested three new ideas, including your suggestion of a field that chooses instead of pushes. Then, for the first time, we handed everything to independent reviewers whose only job was to break it. They broke a lot, including claims in Update 1. This page says what fell, what stood, and what is now open.

The short version

  • You were right that one pass is not enough. Of the conclusions drawn from our own simulations, most were withdrawn or narrowed after review.
  • Still standing: no new pushing field can be behind feeling, though the safety margin is thinner than we said.
  • Your "choosing" loophole is real and wider than we first wrote. A tilt as small as one part in a hundred million per event could steer a brain, and nobody has looked for it inside a living one.
  • A new idea, that feeling is the body's valuation at the point it is handed to the planning mind, is interesting but not established.
  • The scoreboard has been reset, with a new row for "an answer we have not thought of yet".

How we work now

You described the method as conjecture and test, and added that no result should be trusted until someone has tried to knock it down. The loop is now four steps:

  1. Conjecture. Propose a possible answer, even a strange one.
  2. Test. Work out what would follow if it were true, and check with a calculation, a simulation, or published evidence.
  3. Red team. A fresh reviewer who has not seen our reasoning tries to break the result: reruns the code with fairer opponents, checks the arithmetic, looks for what we missed. ("Red team" is borrowed from security testing: the people paid to attack your own defences.)
  4. Record. Only then does it go in the ledger, marked survived, weakened, or withdrawn.

Three reviewers ran this round: one on the physics, one on the simulations and animal evidence, one on the overall logic. Where a reviewer reported new numbers we re-ran the most important ones ourselves before accepting them.

NEW · YOUR SUGGESTION

A field that chooses instead of pushes

Update 1 showed that a weak field cannot shove warm, wet brain matter around. Your notes pointed out a way past that. At the smallest scale, events are truly random: a particle can go one way or the other with equal energy. Picking one costs nothing. A field that tilted those coin-flips would not be a force. It would be a change to the rule of chance itself.

Is there anything in the brain for it to choose between?

The worry was that the brain's randomness is just heat, not true quantum chance. Published work suggests the worry is misplaced. In water at body temperature, molecules lose track of where they are, in the quantum sense, in less than one collision. The jiggling that opens and closes a nerve cell's switches is quantum chance, magnified. And the brain magnifies further: one extra nerve impulse triggers about 28 more. published

Would the brain's chaos scramble the nudges?

The brain is chaotic: tiny differences grow fast. We first tested a chooser that nudges once and found its influence faded to nothing within eight steps. We concluded a chooser would have to simulate the whole brain. The reviewer showed that was an artefact. If the chooser nudges at every step instead of once, chaos works for it:

Steps before the decisionNudge onceNudge every step

853%99.8%

How often a chooser that knows the wiring gets the outcome it wants, with a 5% tilt. 50% means no influence. We re-ran the reviewer's version and got the same numbers.

What the chooser still needs is to know which events count toward which outcome. That is a lot to ask of a law of nature, but it is no longer "impossible in practice".

What has been tested?

  • People trying to influence quantum devices: hundreds of studies. Any effect is below 3 in 100,000, shrinks as studies get bigger, and the best-designed study (12,571 people) supports zero. But most had nothing really pleasant or painful at stake, and a chooser might only work on events inside the brain it belongs to.
  • Events inside living nerve tissue: only checked to within 1 to 10 percent, and only in dishes or under anaesthesia.
  • Awake compared with anaesthetised: never done.

We also first wrote that choosing without tilting the odds would do nothing. The reviewer showed that is wrong too: a chooser could leave every single coin at exactly 50:50 and still steer, by changing which coins come up together. That would be invisible to every test done so far.

Where this stands. Open. The window is a tilt between about 1 in 100 million and 1 in 100, inside an awake brain. Nothing points to it being real, and it would carry heavy costs (it would in principle allow faster-than-light signalling, and no proposal says how big the tilt should be). But we cannot rule it out, and no experiment proposed so far would close more than a small part of the window. Designing one that could is on next round's list.

NEW CONJECTURE

Is feeling the point where the body reports to the planner?

Update 1 left a puzzle. Your idea says feeling is the motivator. But the brain's "go and get it" signal and its pleasure signal are separate parts. And a plain good/bad signal exists in machines that surely feel nothing.

A striking rat experiment suggested a way through. published

  • Train a hungry rat to press a lever for food. Then feed it until full. Put it back: it keeps pressing. Its planning system has not been told the food is no longer wanted.
  • Let it taste the food once while full, away from the lever. Now it stops pressing. The planner needed the experience to update.
  • Yet the rat's automatic reactions update instantly. A signal for disgustingly salty water becomes attractive the moment the rat is short of salt, before it has tasted anything.

So there are two systems. An automatic one always knows what the body needs. A planning one finds out only by experiencing. The conjecture: feeling is the body's valuation at the moment it crosses to the planner. That would make your formula more than a sum. Feeling would be how the body's needs become visible to thought at all.

How it fared under attack

  • We claimed only a two-system design fits the lab findings. The reviewer built a one-system design that fits them all. withdrawn
  • "Experience" in the rat work only means the rat touched the food. Nothing shows it had to be felt.
  • In one small study, people worked hard for a morphine dose too small for them to feel. If that was deliberate action, the conjecture is wrong.
  • There is a risk of circularity: we measure "felt" by what people report, and reporting is done by the planner.

Where this stands. The two-way split in how value is handled is solid. That the felt/unfelt line falls exactly there is an untested idea. It does sharpen the key question: can the body value something without it being felt? If no, feeling is ancient and everywhere, down to worms. If yes, feeling needs a planner and is much rarer. Which animals and machines can feel turns on this.

CHECKED AGAINST DATA

Two more things we learned

The usual tests for animal feeling cannot draw a line. Researchers look for signs such as paying a cost to avoid something harmful, flexible learning, and anxiety that responds to anti-anxiety drugs. Across 22 kinds of animal these turn up nearly everywhere anyone has looked, including a worm with 302 nerve cells. Either feeling is nearly universal, or these tests detect the good/bad signal and not whether it is felt. The one test our conjecture says would matter (the lever experiment above) has been run properly in rats and humans only.

Your formula may need a third part, or may not. Plainly seeing a grey wall is neither a feeling of good or bad nor a thought. Some patients with deep brain damage report a mind that is "a total blank", no thoughts, no feelings, not even boredom, yet they are awake and aware. That suggests consciousness = being awake and aware + feeling + thought. The reviewer pointed out this is not forced: "neutral" might be the zero point on the feeling scale, the way zero is still a temperature. Open.

Next round: new conjectures

The reviewers also proposed ideas we had not considered. Each has a test it could fail.

  1. Feeling is not one thing. If pain, hunger, itch and pleasure switch off at different depths of anaesthesia, "where does sentience come from" is several questions.
  2. There is only one valuing system, and "felt" just means "strong enough to be noticed".
  3. Feeling is an interrupt. A planner that does one thing at a time needs something that can break in. Intensity would track how urgently to switch, not how much is at stake.
  4. One feeler per bottleneck. Wherever all of a creature's competing wants are finally settled in one place, there is one feeler. Split-brain patients and octopus arms are the test.
  5. The chooser, properly. What measurement inside an awake brain could detect a chooser that keeps every coin fair?
  6. Feeling is what a mind cannot describe about its own valuing. A simulation in which the thinker can look at its feelings through a narrow or wide window.
  7. Feeling bridges time. It keeps an event "live" until its consequences arrive.
