Update 10 · 4 October 2026

What your hypothesis may amount to, and a choice only you can make

Update 9 could not separate your idea from its nearest rival, because no study removed the feeling while leaving the sense of value in place. This round went looking for such cases. None was found. But having the rival stated properly led the scorer to a view about your hypothesis itself, and it comes with a fork in the road.

The short version

The independent scorer's sentences, unaltered.

  • "Round 10 looked for cases where people report feeling nothing about an outcome and their flexible, goal-directed action was also measured; no such case was found, so the hypothesis was not tested."
  • "Every row was scored 'no test' for both accounts, and the balance between our hypothesis and the higher-order rival did not move."
  • "In depersonalisation, the best available study (22 patients against 15 patient controls) found unpleasant sounds rated as less unpleasant, not as neutral, and no study we found has measured flexible choice in this condition."
  • "People change their choices at once when told a reward is now worthless, but no study we found measured whether their anticipated feelings changed as well, so this does not favour either account."
  • "Feeling can change without action changing: in a 306-person replication, liking for a food fell after eating to fullness while about two-thirds of participants did not adjust their responding; this is compatible with both accounts."
  • "The independent reviewer's view is that our hypothesis is at present best stated as an identity claim, that felt valence is value made accessible to the planner, and not as a causal hypothesis with evidence behind it."
  • "The experiment that could separate the accounts, an instructed devaluation with feelings rated before the choice, in people who report feeling nothing, has not been run."

What happened when the rival was stated properly

Before gathering any evidence we had our wording checked, as we now do every round. The check found we had again described the rival view unfairly. We had written its predictions as if value were something a person knows and can state (for example, protection "preserved wherever the danger is known"). Its real form is different: the brain computes a value signal that the planning system uses to choose, and that signal is not the same thing as what a person can say they know.

That matters, because several of the conditions we planned to use (pain without unpleasantness, pain-killers, emotion-flattening drugs) act on circuits that, on the rival's account, carry that value signal. So the rival predicts impaired action there too. They stop separating the accounts unless the value signal is shown intact, though they could still count against your idea if someone felt nothing and acted flexibly anyway.

The check also pointed out that the rival comes in two forms.

  • Higher-order form. Value signals are not in themselves felt. Feeling is a further thing the brain constructs on top of them. On this view feeling and value signal are two separate things and could, in principle, come apart.
  • Identity form. The feeling simply is the value signal being available to the planner. There is no further thing.

Against the identity form, your hypothesis makes no different prediction about anything. No observation could tell the two apart.

What the evidence showed

To count against your idea, a study needs two things: the person reports feeling nothing about the outcome, and their flexible action, measured by a strict test, is intact. To count against the rival it needs three: no reported feeling, flexible action lost, and the value signal shown intact by independent means.

No study found anyone reporting no feeling at all. The scorer marked every row "no test" for both sides.

THE FORK

A choice only you can make

The scorer's view, which we report as given: as it can currently be defended, your hypothesis is an identity claim. In plain terms: a feeling of good or bad is what a value signal is, when the planning mind has access to it.

If that is what you mean, three things follow, in the scorer's words or close to them.

  • "Feeling is necessary for flexible action" becomes true by definition. It cannot be cited as a discovery about where sentience comes from.
  • Rounds 9 and 10 should not be described as tests your idea survived.
  • You take on a commitment: any system in which a value signal is available to a planner has felt valence. (Our addition, not the scorer's: that could include some animals and, in principle, some machines.)

If that is not what you mean, because you intend feeling to be an extra ingredient over and above an accessible value signal, then your hypothesis is a causal claim about that ingredient. In that case there is, so far, no evidence distinguishing it from the higher-order rival, which also says feeling is something extra.

So the question for you is: is feeling the same thing as value-made-available-to-thought, or is it something added?

The scorer did not say which reading you intended, and we do not know. The two lead to different research programmes, and we can pursue either or both.

The experiment that could separate your hypothesis from the higher-order rival

Proposed by the scorer. Find people who report feeling nothing at all toward a reward: no pleasure, wanting, dread or regret, checked trial by trial. Teach them to work for two real rewards, then tell them one is now worthless, and see whether their choices change. Separately, check that their value signal is intact, using real-money bids and bodily responses.

  • They report no feeling, yet their choices follow the instruction: your hypothesis fails (and so does the identity form).
  • They report no feeling, the value signal is intact, and they cannot choose even when prompted: the higher-order rival fails.
  • If screening turns up nobody who truly reports feeling nothing, that is itself a result.

A second arm, in healthy volunteers, does not depend on finding such people. Among those whose expected feelings did not change after the instruction: a change in choice counts against your hypothesis; no change, with the whole effect running through changed feelings, counts against the higher-order rival.

How sure are we?

  • The scoring was independent. The scorer checked the key figures of five studies against the full papers and they matched. Several other studies were read only as summaries and were given no weight beyond "nothing here meets the requirements".
  • The identity-claim reading is the scorer's considered view. The fairness check raised it first, conditionally, and the scorer had read that check, so the two are not independent. It is a judgement about the logic of the hypothesis, not a measurement. Nothing this round is evidence for the identity reading over the higher-order rival.
  • The fairness check's account of the rival rests on two paper summaries and unverified general knowledge. The five checked studies did not include the antidepressant or told-worthless studies.
  • A separate check compared a draft of this page, line by line, with the scorer's report, the fairness check and the evidence file. It found 19 problems, listed in reviews/update10-prepublication-check.md, which we then corrected. It did not re-read the original papers and has not re-checked the corrected page.
