Update 14 · 4 October 2026

Two predictions, written down first, passed their tests — and what they show looks like lighter sleep, not a signature of experience

Last time, four studies of people woken from sleep found no reliable brain signature of experience. This round followed up the one well-known published claim in the area, that slow brain waves at the back of the head go with not experiencing anything. Two studies: one asked whether the difference really is at the back; the other took a lead a reviewer stumbled on and tested it once, on recordings that played no part in finding it. That test passed, narrowly. The checks made afterwards suggested a different reading, which became a third study with its own prediction; that passed clearly, and its review then corrected what it means.

The short version

Each line is an independent reviewer's sentence, unaltered.

  • "We wrote down one prediction in advance and tested it once on four sets of sleep recordings that had not been used to find it: 502 awakenings."
  • "Brain-wave power in the slow range, measured roughly half a minute before waking, was lower before people reported an experience than before they reported none; by the rule fixed in advance, the prediction counts as supported (effect −0.27, p about 0.03)."
  • "The result is modest and fragile: no single dataset shows it alone, most of the weight comes from one dataset, and removing either of two datasets pushes it past the conventional threshold."
  • "Checks made afterwards show the difference is not specific to slow waves: power was lower at all frequencies, more so at faster ones, and the slow-wave difference disappears once overall power is accounted for."
  • "So the result fits 'lighter overall sleep goes with reported experience' better than 'slow waves switch experience off'; this reading is after the fact."
  • "One prediction was written down in advance and tested once on 515 awakenings from three sets of recordings; it was supported (effect −0.38, p about 0.0003), and an independent recomputation from the raw recordings gave the same answer."
  • "Looked at band by band, power below about 16 Hz was lower before reports of experience; power above 16 Hz was not, and leaned slightly higher."
  • "So the evidence does not show the brain 'quieter at all speeds'; it shows less slow and medium-speed activity, as in lighter sleep."
  • "This is an association half a minute before waking, not evidence about what causes experience."

Words used on this page

Slow waves are the large, slow ripples (one to four a second) that dominate deep sleep. Power is how strong the activity is at a given speed. An effect of −0.27 means: comparing each person only with themselves, in the same stage of sleep, the measure was about a quarter of that person's usual spread lower before "I was experiencing something" than before "nothing". That is small. p is how often shuffling the answers at random would give a difference at least this big; below 0.05 is the conventional pass mark, and it is a low bar. A dataset is one laboratory's set of recordings; an awakening is one occasion of waking a sleeper and asking. A 95% interval is the range the true value plausibly lies in. Correction for multiple testing means raising the bar when several things are tested at once, since one of them may pass by luck. High-density means many recording sites (here 256); posterior means towards the back of the head. Registered or committed means written into the project's dated history before the data were looked at.

STUDY 5

Is the difference at the back of the head?

A well-known 2017 study reported that when slow waves are weaker over a "hot zone" at the back of the brain, sleepers woken moments later report having been experiencing something (our summary; we have not re-checked the paper's wording). If that is right, the place matters, which would be a real clue about where experience comes from. Study 1 had used only eleven recording sites. Here we used every site on the scalp, in three datasets including the original one, and asked: is the difference bigger at the back than at the front?

  • "Using every scalp channel in three sleep datasets (522 awakenings; 64 people who gave both kinds of report), slow-wave power before reports of experience was lower by about 0.18 within-person standard deviations at the front and 0.17 at the back; neither difference was statistically significant (p = 0.08 and 0.10), and none of our six measures would survive correction for multiple testing."
  • "Front and back slow-wave power rose and fell together almost perfectly from one awakening to the next (correlations of 0.90 to 0.97), so these are better read as one whole-scalp measure than as two separate findings."
  • "The back-minus-front difference was +0.04 (95% interval −0.17 to +0.25): we found no evidence that the reduction is larger at the back, but the three laboratories pointed in different directions (−0.16, +0.34, +0.21)."
  • "In the original high-density dataset the difference leaned towards the back (−0.16, 95% interval −0.44 to +0.12), which is compatible both with no front–back difference and with a modest posterior one."
  • "This is a test at the scalp, not of the 'posterior hot zone' claim itself, which concerns sources inside the brain; in a simulation of our method only about a third to two-thirds of a back-only change showed up in the back-minus-front measure."
  • "A planned confirmation of the front-versus-back study could not be run: only 6 people gave both kinds of report, and 8 were required."

In plain terms: at the scalp, we could not see that the back of the head is special. That does not refute the published claim, which is about sources inside the brain that scalp recordings blur together.

STUDY 6

The lead: look half a minute earlier

While attacking Study 5, the reviewer tried a different time window and found the slow-wave difference looked larger 34 to 14 seconds before waking than in the last 20 seconds. We make no claim that the earlier window is better; that comparison was not a test. Something found by trying several things is a lead, not a finding. So we wrote down one measure, one prediction and one pass mark, committed them, and only then ran the measure on four datasets that had played no part in finding the lead. The fourth was still downloading when the plan was fixed and was not opened until the download was verified complete.

  • "We wrote down one prediction in advance and tested it once on four sets of sleep recordings that had not been used to find it: 502 awakenings."
  • "Brain-wave power in the slow range, measured roughly half a minute before waking, was lower before people reported an experience than before they reported none; by the rule fixed in advance, the prediction counts as supported (effect −0.27, p about 0.03)."
  • "The result is modest and fragile: no single dataset shows it alone, most of the weight comes from one dataset, and removing either of two datasets pushes it past the conventional threshold."
  • "In the fourth dataset the recordings stop up to a minute before waking, so its timing is imprecise; it is too small to matter either way."

This is the first prediction in the project's own studies to pass a test set in advance. It passed narrowly. Of the 502 awakenings, only 319 come from a person-and-sleep-stage combination with both kinds of answer, and only those can contribute.

What the result seems to mean

We expected, if the test passed, to be able to say "slow waves go with the absence of experience". Two independent reviewers then checked whether the difference was really about slow waves. These checks were made after seeing results, so they are weaker evidence than the test itself.

  • "Checks made afterwards show the difference is not specific to slow waves: power was lower at all frequencies, more so at faster ones, and the slow-wave difference disappears once overall power is accounted for."
  • "Time of night explains only part of it, about a quarter."
  • "So the result fits 'lighter overall sleep goes with reported experience' better than 'slow waves switch experience off'; this reading is after the fact."

Before opening the fourth dataset we had written down what each of the two readings predicted there. It turned out to have only seven usable person-and-stage combinations, and could not tell them apart.

One more caution from the first review: the test's verdict depended on how the moment of waking was located in two of the datasets. With the fourth dataset added, locating waking the alternative way in those two datasets still passes (effect −0.26, p 0.038). Other reasonable settings of the waking rule in the largest dataset, which gave p 0.06 to 0.08 on three datasets, were not rechecked. Leaving out single participants pushed p above 0.05 in 4 or 5 of 34 cases.

STUDY 7

Testing the new reading: is it everything except the slowest waves?

The checks above suggested the real difference was in overall power at faster speeds (4 to 40 ripples a second), not in slow waves. That was found after the fact, so we wrote it down as a prediction and ran it once on the three Study 5 datasets. Those have not had this measure computed in this window before, but they are where the slow-wave lead came from, so they are not fully fresh.

  • "One prediction was written down in advance and tested once on 515 awakenings from three sets of recordings; it was supported (effect −0.38, p about 0.0003), and an independent recomputation from the raw recordings gave the same answer."
  • "The measure was dominated by slower rhythms: more than half of it is 4–8 Hz power."
  • "Looked at band by band, power below about 16 Hz was lower before reports of experience; power above 16 Hz was not, and leaned slightly higher."
  • "So the evidence does not show the brain 'quieter at all speeds'; it shows less slow and medium-speed activity, as in lighter sleep."
  • "The difference does not appear to come from movement or muscle artefact, time of night, the choice of reference, or any single person or dataset."
  • "In two of the three datasets part of the difference tracks how the awakenings were scheduled; the cleanest dataset is one from which a similar finding had already been published."
  • "Across all seven datasets, mixing the four that suggested the idea with the three that tested it, the 4–8 Hz reduction appears in every one; faster activity goes different ways in different datasets."
  • "This is an association half a minute before waking, not evidence about what causes experience."

Two corrections to our own work came out of this review. First, our measure was badly named: we had called it power "at all faster speeds", but it is mostly 4–8 Hz, and when each speed is given equal weight the difference in the test datasets is −0.06 (p 0.60). The Study 6 reviewer's sentence that power was lower "at all frequencies, more so at faster ones" describes the four Study 6 datasets; it does not hold in the three test datasets. Second, the step meant to find the moment of waking did nothing in two of the three datasets, which have no channel our code recognised as a muscle channel; the reviewer checked that those recordings do end at the awakening, so the result stands.

How this sits with published work, from the reviewer's memory and not re-checked: the 2017 study reported less slow activity and more fast activity before reports of dreaming. Our slow and medium-speed result agrees with it; our data do not show the fast-activity increase reliably, though the three test datasets lean that way. The cleanest of our test datasets is that study's own data, so on this point we have partly re-found something known.

Why this matters for the big question

Our working picture is that feeling comes first and thought serves it. A sharp, local brain signature of "experience on / experience off" would have been a foothold for asking what that signature does. What we have instead is a modest difference across the whole scalp: less slow and medium-speed activity (below about 16 Hz) half a minute before awakenings at which people report an experience. That is what lighter sleep looks like, though we have not measured sleep depth directly. It is an association, and a small one: in Update 13, about four-fifths of the variation in reports was explained by nothing we measured.

The honest position: in open scalp recordings we have now found one difference that passes tests set in advance, and it looks like "less deeply asleep", not like a signal specific to experience or to one place on the head. Whether anything is left once sleep depth is properly accounted for is the next question.

How sure are we?

  • The three-dataset interim number was reproduced by an independent reviewer from the raw recordings. The final four-dataset number was recomputed by a second reviewer from our per-awakening values, with the fourth dataset re-derived from raw recordings. Their after-the-fact numbers (the table above, apart from its first row) were each computed by one reviewer and not checked by anyone else.
  • The plan, an interim result on three datasets (−0.26, p about 0.045), an amendment and the final result are in the project history in that order. The interim result was recorded before the fourth dataset was opened so that it could not quietly be preferred. Timestamps cannot prove nobody peeked.
  • The way Study 6 pooled its datasets followed its own plan but differs from Studies 1 and 5, which required at least eight people with both kinds of answer per dataset. Under that older rule only one dataset qualifies, and it alone gives −0.23, p 0.10.
  • In the largest dataset the answer was a bare yes or no, and the moment of waking had to be estimated from muscle activity. In the fourth dataset "no experience" includes "can't remember".
  • Before the plan, a reviewer had seen a hint in the largest dataset that an earlier window gave a larger difference.
  • A p of about 0.03 on a single test (Study 6) is weak evidence by itself. Study 7's result is much stronger statistically, but its datasets are not fully fresh and its cleanest dataset had already produced a similar published finding.
  • Study 7's headline number was recomputed by an independent reviewer from the raw recordings. That reviewer's own after-the-fact figures (the band table, the scheduling and artefact checks) have not been checked by anyone else.

Next

  1. Is anything left once sleep depth is accounted for? If the difference is simply lighter sleep, standard measures of depth should carry the same information and nothing should remain. If something remains, that is the interesting part.
  2. The fast-activity question. Datasets disagree about activity above 16 Hz. Whether that disagreement comes from how each laboratory scheduled its awakenings can be checked.
  3. The open question to you from Update 10 still stands: is feeling the same thing as a value signal available to thought, or something added?
