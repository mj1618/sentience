Update 8 · 4 October 2026

An audit of everything so far: what we overstated, and a change of direction

This round we asked a fresh reviewer to go back over every claim the project currently treats as standing, and to check whether our public summaries matched the reviews behind them. They mostly did not. This update reports what was wrong, what has been changed, and where the work goes next.

The short version

Every line here is taken from a reviewer's or adjudicator's report. This page was itself checked against those reports before publication.

  • Of the eight items Update 7 listed as "reviewed and standing", one matched its review, six were stated more strongly than the review, and one had never been reviewed.
  • The percentage scoreboard was, in the auditor's word, decoration. Its numbers were not connected to any test. It has been removed.
  • The striking observation in Update 7, patients signalling pain under anaesthesia, is weaker than we reported: many of those patients also squeezed to nonsense statements, so it does not establish how many actually felt pain.
  • The auditor judged that the project had drifted from your question, and that the evidence bearing most directly on your hypothesis has not been reviewed in eight rounds. The next round goes back to it.

THIS ROUND'S NEW WORK

Pain under anaesthesia: a closer look

We followed up the anaesthesia observation with predictions written in advance and independent scoring. The scorer supplied the sentences we are allowed to use, and here they are unaltered.

  • "In two multicentre studies, about 5% and 11% of patients squeezed a hand to spoken prompts shortly after the breathing tube was placed under general anaesthesia, and almost none remembered it afterwards."
  • "Roughly half of those who responded also squeezed when asked if they were in pain, but many squeezed to nonsense statements too, so this does not establish how many actually felt pain."
  • "These tests were done before surgery began, and no study has recorded brain activity at the moments pain was signalled or related those signals to pain-killer dose."
  • "The evidence therefore did not separate the deep-first account from the cortical account; an independent adjudicator scored the overall shift as none."
  • "The cortical account was again tested through a frontal EEG proxy its proponents would not accept, so the apparent difficulties for it in this round should not be counted against it."

The last sentence describes a mistake we have now made in four rounds running: describing a rival idea in a form its own supporters would reject. From the next round an independent check of the wording happens before any evidence is scored.

TWO IDEAS OF OURS, DOWNGRADED

The "report barrier" and "experiments cannot pick a winner"

In Updates 4 and 6 we offered two general lessons: that evidence from anything unable to report cannot separate the theories, and that rival claims about what feeling is all predict the same reports. We labelled both as our own unreviewed thinking. The auditor has now reviewed them and judged both to be over-generalisations.

  • Our "nothing decided" results had duller explanations: we had mis-stated the rival ideas, we were often reading summaries and not full papers, and the decisive experiments have simply not been done yet. That is a practical gap, not a wall.
  • Theories do differ on which brain conditions go along with a report. That is exactly how one marker was struck off in Update 7.
  • Other identity questions in science (what heat is, what a gene is) were settled by intervening and then choosing the best explanation.

The auditor's verdict: fair as a description of our first seven rounds, overreach as a claim about the question, and in practice an excuse.

THE AUDITOR'S JUDGEMENT OF THE PROJECT

Most and least valuable so far

Most valuable: the method of writing predictions first and having them scored independently, together with the public record of corrections; two concrete experiments proposed by the scorers; and the narrow debunkings that held (the three brain regions; the much-cited xenon and precognition evidence being far weaker than usually claimed).

Least valuable: the scoreboard; the simulations, every one of which was withdrawn; and the "chooser" and lottery thread, which took a great deal of effort on an unlikely option and ended in a test that says nothing about brains.

Drift. Your question was where feeling comes from, with the working idea that feeling is the motivator and thought works out how to satisfy it. Rounds 2 to 7 went to fields, choosers, lottery balls and markers of seeing and dreaming. The published findings that bear most directly on your idea (people who cannot feel pain, people who lose their inner drive, the difference between wanting and liking) were listed in Update 1 and have never been put through review.

How sure are we?

  • This page reports reviewers' findings and quotes their wording. It was checked against their reports by a separate auditor before being published.
  • The audit itself checked two sources directly; the rest of its work was comparing our summaries with our own reviews.

Next round: back to your hypothesis

Already under way, with the predictions written and saved before any evidence was gathered, and the wording being checked for fairness first.

Is felt good-and-bad what motivates? Your idea is set against two named rivals: that an unfelt "wanting" signal does the motivating, and that simply knowing what is valuable is enough. Nine situations are tested, among them: rewards shown too briefly to be seen, people working for a drug dose too small to feel, pain felt without unpleasantness, people born unable to feel pain, loss of pleasure versus loss of drive, and patients who know what they should do but have lost the feeling that goes with it.
