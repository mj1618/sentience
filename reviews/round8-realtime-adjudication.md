## 3. What the isolated-forearm evidence shows about felt pain without recall

**Shows**

- A minority of patients squeeze a hand to spoken prompts shortly after intubation:
  4.6% (12/260; abstract) and 11% (37/338, ages 18–40; full text).
- Among those responders, 5/12 and 18/37 squeezed after a pain prompt.
- Later explicit recall of this was essentially absent: 0/253 (Sanders); 1 definite in 338
  (Lennertz), and that person was not a responder. Forced-choice word memory was at chance.
- Russell 1993 (abstract): 20 of 32 patients indicated pain *during surgery*; 3 recalled
  on prompting.

**Does not show**

- **Timing.** The two large studies tested once, after intubation and before incision. The
  stimulus is laryngoscopy/intubation (and possibly the tourniquet), not surgery. Only the
  small Russell series (abstract-only) concern surgery.
- **Depth.** No EEG exists for the 18 Lennertz pain-signallers. In Sanders, responders as a
  group had *higher* monitor values than non-responders (54 vs 42). No study reports depth
  for pain-signallers. Pain signalled "in the unconscious range" is not established.
- **How pain was asked.** One yes-only prompt ("if you are in pain squeeze my hand two
  times"), any squeeze counted, no "not in pain" control, no rating, unblinded assessor.
- **Base rates.** 11 of 24 early responders squeezed to a nonsense statement; 3 of 7
  comprehension answers were wrong. A 49% squeeze rate after the pain prompt is not clearly
  above non-specific squeezing; it is an upper bound, not an incidence of felt pain.
- **Recall.** Recall tests asked about intraoperative events generally; no study tested
  recall of the pain signal specifically in the pain-signallers.
- **Relation to opioid dose or stimulus intensity.** Not reported anywhere.

Net: the IFT data establish that command-following without later recall happens under
intended general anaesthesia. That some of it involves felt pain is plausible and
unconfirmed.

## 4. Overall shift

**None.** Ratio ≈ 1:1. On the scenario's own wording there is at most a faint lean toward
D on row 1 (≈1.1:1), resting on abstract-only studies of 6–16 responders using a proxy C's
proponents reject, offset by larger samples pointing the other way. The scenario's own
decisive conditions (pain signals against cortical state, and against opioid versus
hypnotic dose) have no data.
