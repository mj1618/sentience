# Round 9 — Adjudication of scenario 18: is felt valence the motivator?

Independent adjudicator; scored against Part 1b. Spot-checked: Pessiglione 2007, 2008 and Berkovitch 2022 in full text; Lamb 1991 and Skora 2023 abstracts (Lamb is paywalled). The evidence file described these accurately.

## 0. Three things that limit every score

1. **Nothing reaches CONFIRMED.** No study in the file both removes felt valence and applies one of criteria 1–4.
2. **"Unfelt" is never established as defined.** The criterion requires chance-level detection *and* no change in reported affect. The masking studies (rows 1–2) measure cue detection only; affect was not measured. They test whether a *seen cue* is needed, not whether a *feeling* is needed. Pessiglione 2008 even instructed subjects to respond "if they had a feeling" they were in a winning trial.
3. **The "automatic" category is wide.** "Responding acquired over repeated reinforced trials" covers almost all operant behaviour, so on row 3 H survives by default classification, not by demonstration.

## 2. Is there a replicated finding that is flexible AND unfelt?

**No.**

- **Pessiglione 2007.** No reward × effort interaction is present, because none could be: effort demand was not varied and subjects could not decline. Force rising with coin value is "response vigour scaling with cue magnitude alone" — automatic under the fixed definition. It is not criterion 4. The one replication found (Berkovitch 2022) is marginal, with subjects measurably above chance.
- **Lamb 1991.** Neither shown to be repetition-acquired nor shown to be flexible; the question was not asked. It is the nearest threat to H (3,000 presses for a delayed injection is not obviously a habit), but it is five people, one abstract, unreplicated.
- **Subliminal instrumental learning.** The original positive result does not survive trial-wise awareness checks or registered replication. It would not have met the criterion even if it had.

H therefore does not fail. It also has not been put at risk: no study found ran a criterion-meeting test under verified absence of feeling.

## 3. H versus W; H versus K\*

**H vs W: none to slight, toward W — roughly 1.2 : 1, inside the noise.** Once W is stated as Berridge holds it, the two barely conflict: W does not claim unfelt wanting drives planned action, and H does not deny wanting–liking dissociation. W made the riskier predictions (rows 3, 6, 7) and they held on weak evidence; H was merely compatible.

**H vs K\*: agreed, underdetermined (1 : 1).** Rows 1–2 manipulate cue visibility, not feeling, so they bear most directly on *access*, K\*'s variable. Nothing shows feeling adds anything beyond an accessible value representation.

## 4. What can be said about "sentience is the motivator; thought works out how"

- **(a) Supported.** Only negatively: no replicated case of flexible, goal-directed human action driven by a reward the person demonstrably neither perceived nor felt. Where feeling is lost or blunted (rows 4, 5, 8, 9), self-initiated action is impaired — weak evidence, equally predicted by K\*.
- **(b) Contradicted.** Nothing contradicts the core claim. The permissive half ("automatic behaviour can run on unfelt valuation") is strained: with strict awareness checks, unseen rewards produced no learning, and one preregistered study [V-abs] found no masked triggering of habits either.
- **(c) Untested.** The causal claim itself — that the *feeling*, as distinct from the accessible value representation that comes with it, does the motivating. No study dissociates the two.
- **(d) Difference from existing positions.** Small. Dickinson's hedonic interface theory already holds that goal-directed action depends on consciously experienced affective value of the outcome; H is close to a restatement of it. Berridge already grants that cognitive, goal-declared wanting is distinct from incentive salience. What H adds is the word "necessary" and the identification of the value signal with sentience. Neither addition has been tested.

## 5. Experiments that could refute H

1. **Sub-subjective dose with a criterion probe.** Double-blind, preregistered, n≥30. Two novel options, one paired with a low active dose (opioid in experienced users, or caffeine/nicotine), one with placebo. Require drug discrimination at chance and equivalence-tested affect ratings. Then test choice via a never-reinforced response, and whether responding falls as effort price rises. Reliable criterion-meeting preference for the active option refutes H.
2. **Masked reward × visible effort with a decline option.** Masked reward magnitude crossed with signalled effort level; accept or decline each trial; trial-by-trial awareness and affect ratings. A reward × effort interaction in acceptance at d′=0 refutes H (and K\*).

## 6. Permitted summary sentences (verbatim only)

1. We tested the idea that feeling good or bad is needed for flexible, self-initiated action against two established rival accounts, using rules fixed before scoring.
2. We found no replicated study in which people acted flexibly toward a reward they demonstrably could not perceive or feel, so the idea was not refuted.
3. It was also not confirmed: no study we found removed feeling and then applied a strict test of flexible action, so the idea was never put at real risk.
4. The best-known experiments on unseen rewards show only that people squeeze harder or respond faster, and the claim that people can learn from unseen rewards did not hold up in a registered replication.
5. The evidence does not separate our idea from the view that flexible action needs an accessible representation of value, whether or not it is felt; most of the experiments hid the cue rather than removing the feeling.
6. Against Berridge's account the evidence moved slightly, if at all, in his favour, and our idea is close to a position Anthony Dickinson has held for decades.
7. One small 1991 study, in which five people worked for a morphine dose they could not distinguish from placebo, remains an untested possible counterexample.
