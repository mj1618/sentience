## Accepted and built on

**Three kinds of chooser**, which we had lumped together:

| Kind | Aims using | Testable? |
|---|---|---|
| Future-aimed | the outcome that has not happened yet | Yes, exact baseline |
| Hidden-fact-aimed | present facts the brain has not sensed | Yes, same way |
| State-local | only the brain's present state | No: identical to "biology sets the odds". **Unscored**, since no data can move it |

**Randomised read-out test** (reviewer's design): record brain activity, *then* draw at
random which pattern counts as a win. Any ordinary brain scores exactly 50%.

**Our addition (fetched, Europe PMC):** this has in effect been run at whole-person
scale. Bem-style precognition experiments ask people to choose before a random draw
decides which choice is rewarded. A 2025 multi-lab replication (Walleczek et al.,
PLoS One) reports 26,483 participants and 420,472 trials with no effect (one sub-study
at 49.65% ± 0.14, below chance, judged a false positive). That bounds a future-aimed
chooser's effect on choices to about ±0.2%, which maps to a per-event tilt below
~2×10⁻⁵ (10⁴ events pooled) down to ~6×10⁻¹¹ (10¹⁵ pooled). The future-aimed and
hidden-fact-aimed choosers are closed across essentially the whole window.

## Lessons (process)

1. Our scenario verdicts ran stronger than the briefs they cited, three times. A verdict
   may not be stronger than its source's.
2. The round 2 rule (stronger opponent and a sweep before citing a simulation) was not
   followed for the interrupt model. It is now a checklist item in `LEDGER.md`.
3. Abstract numbers can be extrapolations. Read the methods before computing from them.
