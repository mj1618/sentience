## 3. Row 6: do ordinary people have the intuition?

The evidence supports a narrow statement, weakly. (a) No representative-sample estimate
exists. (b) The only studies aimed directly at the question (Díaz 2021, abstract-only and
unverified here; Sytsma and Ozdemir 2019, title recalled) conclude it is not widespread.
(c) Laypeople do judge science less able to explain states with first-person access
(Gottlieb and Lombrozo, verified abstract), but that is a relative reluctance bundled with
human exceptionality and free will, not the specific "extra inner quality" claim.
(d) Zombie intuitions move with wording (Fischer and Sytsma). (e) Most lay responses
cross-culturally are not dualist, and dualism is not the puzzle intuition anyway.
(f) Chalmers's "easy to elicit" is asserted without data.

Conclusion: it is **not established** that ordinary people spontaneously hold the
intuition, and the thin direct evidence leans against it being widespread. It is equally
**not established** that it is a product of philosophical training: no study compares
people before and after such training, and a diffuse lay sense that inner life resists
scientific explanation does appear. Strength: weak, direction mildly against
"widespread". "Everyone feels the mystery" is an open assumption, not a datum.

**None.** The only movement is a **slight** loss of confidence in the premise both share (row 6), and a

## 5. Is I versus Q testable?

Not directly. Realists such as Chalmers accept the same mechanisms for why we *judge*
feelings mysterious, so any finding about the judgement is absorbable by Q. What is
testable is the mechanism of the intuition and particular illusionist theories.

1. **Preregistered ineffability-by-skill study.** Build a validated scale for how
   indescribable and scientifically inexplicable *ordinary* feelings (a pain, a colour)
   seem. Administer to a representative multi-country lay sample, philosophy graduates,
   and long-term meditators, plus a randomised introspection-training arm with pre and
   post measures. Dennett-style I: scores fall with skill and training. Kammerer-style I
   and Q: no fall, or a rise. This also yields the missing row-6 prevalence figure and
   the training effect.
2. **Self-modelling agent without consciousness talk.** Train a capable language-using
   agent with an attention schema on a corpus purged of mind and consciousness vocabulary,
   then probe open-endedly. Graziano-style I predicts unprompted insistence on an inner
   something it cannot describe. Q predicts nothing either way. Failure would hurt that
   version of I; success would not hurt Q.

## 6. Plain-language summary

Some thinkers say the deep puzzle about feelings (why does pain *feel* like something?)
is a trick of self-perception: the brain keeps only a rough sketch of its own workings,
so its states look simple and beyond words. Others say feelings really do have an inner
quality that no account of brain function captures. We looked for evidence that could
tell these apart and found none that does. People are often wrong about their own minds,
and meditation, illness and drugs can change how feelings seem, but both views expect
that. The most useful thing we learned is uncomfortable for both: nobody has properly
checked whether ordinary people, without being taught philosophy, feel this puzzle at
all, and the few small studies that asked suggest many do not. So the honest answer is
"we cannot tell, and the question may not be settled by experiment". What experiment can
settle is where the sense of mystery comes from and how common it is.
